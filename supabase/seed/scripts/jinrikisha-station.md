Look at the corner in front of you. Two roads meet at a sharp angle, and a building has been made to fit that angle exactly. It is a wedge of brick and plaster with a tower on top. This is the former Jinrikisha Station, at the meeting of Neil Road and Tanjong Pagar Road, and it was built to keep count of a trade that once filled Singapore's streets.

Start with the word. Jinrikisha is Japanese, and it means a man powered carriage. The rickshaw is generally believed to have been invented in Japan around 1869. The idea spread quickly. By 1872 there were about forty thousand in service in Japan, and China saw its first in 1873. In 1880 the first ones reached Singapore, shipped in by way of Shanghai. The rickshaw was a small, light cart with springs and large wheels, and the puller ran between its two shafts. The city took to it fast. Passengers included the well to do and British civil servants and government officials, while the men between the shafts were mostly poor immigrants. One photograph from 1900 shows a rickshaw jam outside the old Police Court on South Bridge Road. The demand was so great that Japan began making cheaper versions.

With so many vehicles on the road, the government wanted order. Accounts say a Jinricksha Ordinance was passed in 1888, and a Jinricksha Department was set up to register, license and manage both the rickshaws and the men who pulled them. Every rickshaw and every puller was meant to be known to the authorities.

This building was the answer to that need. The Municipal Council built it in 1903, although one account says it only began operating in 1904. The design is credited to the Municipal Engineer, Samuel Tomlinson, and the Municipal Architect, D M Craik. It was not just an office. It was the registration centre for rickshaws, and pullers often waited for passengers around the junction outside.

Now look at the building itself. The style is called Edwardian. The brickwork is the first thing to notice, set against white plaster mouldings. The brick was originally left bare, and the walls have since been painted. Find the corner, where the two roads meet. There you should see a curved pediment above, flanked by flat columns called Ionic pilasters. Higher up is a square tower, topped with an eight sided cupola.

Now look for the crest at the front. It is the crest of the Municipal Council, and it shows a lion standing on an island with a palm tree.

So who were the pullers? Most were newcomers from southern China. A steady flow of new arrivals, called singkeh, came because poverty and overpopulation at home were severe. Cantonese, Hengwah, Hockchia and Foochow men are all named among them. Another account says that many came from Fuzhou in Fujian province, and that the Hockchew people dominated the trade. Heritage records say many of these early Chinese immigrants endured harsh lives as pullers. They were usually barefoot, and many wore a conical hat with a flat brim.

Most did not own the vehicle. They rented it from an owner, and there were about one thousand owners by the end of the nineteenth century. Up to the 1920s, many pullers earned only around sixty cents a day, and part of that went on the rent. They lived in cramped cubicles in the shophouses of Chinatown. Rickshaws were accident prone, and pullers often suffered injuries. Many turned to opium to get through the exhaustion.

The loads could be heavy. Until 1904, most rickshaws were two seaters, with iron rimmed wheels, and a single puller might carry a family or a pile of goods. Around that year, lighter single seat models with rubber cushioned wheels appeared, and by 1919 they had replaced the older kind. The government moved against the two seaters in 1911. One account says opposition delayed the law by three years, and that it did little good in the end. Another account says the two seaters disappeared from the streets in the 1920s.

The trade could also turn tense. Martial law was declared in 1897 to end a four day strike by rickshaw workers. Later, a Rickshaw Association was formed in the 1920s to look after the pullers' welfare.

The numbers grew enormous. Sources give different figures. One says about fifty thousand rickshaws by 1920. Another says about fifty thousand pullers in that decade, with only some thirty thousand of them registered and the rest working as a kind of pirate taxi. Either way, tens of thousands of men were pulling people through these streets. Then came the change. Cycle rickshaws, or trishaws, were introduced in 1929, and within six years they outnumbered the pulled kind. In 1947, pulled rickshaws were phased out, and trishaws, trams and buses took over.

Think about the neighbourhood around you, too. Tanjong Pagar is a Malay name that means cape of stakes, probably after the stakes fishermen used to secure fish traps along this shore. The Tanjong Pagar Dock Company was formed in 1864, and wharves were built along the coast as shipping increased. The area was an enclave for thousands of Chinese and Indian dock workers who had migrated to Singapore from the mid nineteenth century. By the 1890s, two and three storey shophouses had been built on nearby Duxton Hill, and the district kept growing around the port.

The station did not stay a rickshaw office. At one point it was a family planning clinic. In 1987 the Urban Redevelopment Authority restored it, and it was one of the first buildings in Tanjong Pagar to be restored. Today it is a conserved building.

Before you walk on, look back along both roads from the narrow end of the corner. This is where one of the busiest working trades in colonial Singapore was written down, licensed and checked, one rickshaw at a time.

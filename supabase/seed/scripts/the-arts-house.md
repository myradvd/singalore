Before you go inside, find the bronze elephant standing on its tall pedestal in front of the building. It is easy to walk past. Keep it in mind, because we will come back to it. The white building behind it is The Arts House. For most of the twentieth century it carried a very different name. Singaporeans knew it as Parliament House, and today many still call it the Old Parliament House.

It is often described as the oldest government building in Singapore. The ground it sits on is older still. When workers refurbished the building in 1989, they found fragments of stoneware and earthenware from the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, a sign that people lived along this stretch of river long before the British arrived. This riverbank is also where Stamford Raffles is thought to have come ashore at the end of January 1819. At the time, the area was home to the Temenggong Abdul Rahman and his followers. A few years later Raffles persuaded them to move to Telok Blangah, because he wanted this land for public use.

That plan is the root of the building's odd early history. In 1826, a Scottish merchant named John Argyle Maxwell, who traded out of Java, began building a grand private house here. Raffles had marked the plot for government use, but his successor, John Crawfurd, gave Maxwell a permit to build anyway. The house was finished in 1827. Its designer was George Drumgoole Coleman. Coleman later became Superintendent of Public Works and designed the Armenian Church and the first St Andrew's Church.

Coleman gave Maxwell a mansion in the Neo Palladian style. But Maxwell never lived in it. Once the authorities realised the house stood on land meant for the government, a compromise was struck. Maxwell kept the property on a very long lease, and the government rented the house from him for five hundred rupees a month. It became a courthouse. Records differ on exactly how ownership changed hands after that, but by 1842 the building belonged to the government and the East India Company.

So for much of the nineteenth century, this was a place of law. The first court sittings were held in a room on the upper floor at the front. Government offices, including the Land Office, shared the space. In 1839 a single storey courthouse went up next door, and the court moved into it. It did not stay. A boatyard nearby made so much noise that the judges went back to the main house. In 1868 the Supreme Court was set up on the premises.

As the colony grew, so did the building, and with each change Coleman's design faded a little more. John McNair carried out major works in the 1870s. In 1901 the building was extended towards the river, and the extension wiped out much of Coleman's original design. In 1909 two courtrooms were rebuilt, and a home for the Attorney General was added. The Supreme Court finally left in 1939, moving into a newly built Supreme Court building. After that, the old house became offices and a government store, and after the Second World War it was used by the Department of Social Welfare.

Its most important chapter came next. In the middle of the 1950s, Singapore was moving towards self government. The old house was renovated by the Public Works Department, and a council chamber was carved out of its interior for a new Legislative Assembly. The building took the name Assembly House. After Singapore won partial internal self government in 1955, the elected assembly met here. The debates that shaped the road to self rule and then independence took place inside these walls. When Singapore became independent in 1965, the building became the home of the national Parliament, and it was known from then on as Parliament House.

Step back and look at the front of the building. What you see now is more Neoclassical than Neo Palladian, with triangular pediments and tall Corinthian pilasters added over the years. But one piece of Coleman's design survives. Look for the Palladian window on the main front: a three part window, with a tall arched opening in the middle and a narrower rectangular opening on each side. The arches at the porch also belong to the first house.

Parliament sat here for more than three decades. By the late 1980s the building was too small, and a new Parliament House was planned right next door. Members moved into it in September 1999, and this building became the Old Parliament House. It had already been made a national monument in February 1992, together with its annex. After several years of restoration, it reopened on 26 March 2004 as The Arts House, sometimes called The Arts House at the Old Parliament.

Inside, the old debating chamber has been kept. It is now a room of around a hundred and fifty seats called The Chamber. Its high coffered ceiling, the Tuscan pilasters along the walls and the old parliamentary seats are still there. In August 2020, during the COVID 19 pandemic, the chamber briefly returned to its old role. To give members room to keep their distance, the opening of Singapore's fourteenth Parliament was split between the new Parliament House and this old one.

Now, back to the elephant. It is a gift from King Chulalongkorn of Siam, the country we now call Thailand. In March 1871 the king landed in Singapore. It was the first time a Siamese monarch had visited a foreign country, and the statue marks that visit. It was cast in bronze in Bangkok. Walk around the pedestal and you will find the same message about his visit written in four languages: English, Siamese, Chinese and Jawi.

The elephant did not start out here. It was first put up on 25 June 1872 in front of the Town Hall, the building now known as Victoria Theatre and Victoria Concert Hall, close by. In 1919, as Singapore marked a hundred years since Raffles arrived, his statue was given that spot. The elephant was moved to a new plot of grass outside what was then the Supreme Court. That is the building in front of you.

So the house and the elephant have been neighbours for over a century. One is a house built for a merchant who never moved in. The other is a gift from a king, moved aside to make room for Raffles. Before you leave, look once more at that Palladian window. It has looked out over the river since 1827, through a courthouse, an assembly, a parliament, and now an arts centre.

Telok Ayer Street is a long way from the sea today. Between here and the water there are whole blocks of offices, roads and reclaimed land. But when this temple was built, the shoreline ran along this street, and many of the people who came to pray here had only just stepped off a boat. This is Thian Hock Keng, one of the oldest Hokkien temples in Singapore, and for generations the spiritual heart of the Hokkien community.

At its centre is Mazu, the goddess of the sea. Many of the early Chinese immigrants to Singapore came from Fujian province on the south eastern coast of China, and many of them were Hokkien. Their journey south was long and dangerous. Those who survived it came ashore at Telok Ayer Bay, and their first act on land was often to give thanks to Mazu for a safe crossing. Others came before setting out again, to ask for her protection on the voyage ahead.

Before there was a temple, there was a small shrine near the shore, set up in the early 1820s. It was modest, but it mattered. As more and more immigrants arrived, the community decided that the goddess deserved a proper home. Money was gathered over many years, and building on a permanent temple began in 1839.

It took three years. The temple was completed in 1842, at an estimated cost of thirty thousand Spanish dollars. Most of the money came from wealthy Hokkien merchants and community leaders. The largest single gift came from Tan Tock Seng, a Malacca born merchant and philanthropist. A granite tablet in the entrance hall still lists the donors, and its inscription makes a point worth noticing: the temple was meant for all Chinese, not only for one dialect group.

Much of what you see was brought from China. Building materials were shipped in, and artisans from Fujian made the carvings and decorations. According to the temple's own history, some of the stone and timber came as ballast in the holds of ships, weight that was needed for the voyage and then put to new use on land. The temple also remembers help from beyond the Chinese community. Indian workers from nearby Chulia Street are said to have helped with the building, and a small carved figure of a man holding up a beam is thought to honour them.

In 1840, while the building work was still going on, a statue of Mazu arrived from China. It was carried through the streets in a grand procession and placed in the main hall as the temple's chief deity. That same year, the Hokkien Huay Kuan, the Hokkien clan association, was set up within the temple grounds. Its aims included running schools, supporting new arrivals from Fujian, and keeping Chinese language and culture alive far from home.

Take a moment to look at the building itself. Thian Hock Keng follows a traditional Hokkien plan of three halls set one behind the other: an entrance hall, a main hall, and a rear hall, with side halls running along the edges. The roofs are the first thing most people notice. Their ridges curve upwards and end in sweeping tips shaped like a swallow's tail, and they are crowded with dragons and other figures. Many of these are made with a technique called jian nian, in which pieces of coloured porcelain are cut and set into the plaster to form a bright mosaic. At the entrance, stone lions and painted door gods stand guard.

Inside, the main hall belongs to Mazu. But she is not alone. At the back of the temple is a shrine to Guanyin, the goddess of mercy. Other altars honour Baosheng Dadi, a god of medicine, and Guan Yu, also known as Lord Guan. Confucius is honoured here too.

The temple was never only a place of worship. In 1849, a small complex called Chongwen Ge was added beside it, led by the merchant Tan Kim Seng with support from Hokkien, Hakka and Teochew donors. It is described as the first Chinese school in Singapore. In the early twentieth century, the Chong Hock Pavilion was built next to the temple, and the Hokkien Huay Kuan later started a girls' school there, one of the earliest Chinese schools for girls on the island.

The temple kept changing as the city around it changed. A renovation in 1906 added a few Western touches, including an ornamental iron gate made in Glasgow and decorative wall tiles. Then, in 1907, came the temple's highest honour. Emperor Guangxu of the Qing dynasty in China sent a plaque and a scroll to Thian Hock Keng. The four characters on the plaque, read as bo jing nan ming, can be translated as gentle waves on the southern seas. It was a fitting blessing for a temple built for sailors and immigrants, and it hangs in the main hall.

Meanwhile, the sea was moving away. From the late nineteenth century onwards, land reclamation pushed the shoreline further and further out, until the temple that once looked across the water found itself looking at streets and buildings.

In 1973, Thian Hock Keng was gazetted as a national monument. A major restoration followed much later, running from 1998 to 2000. In 2001 the work received an honourable mention in the UNESCO Asia Pacific Heritage Awards for Cultural Heritage Conservation.

As you stand in front of the temple, try to picture the scene as it once was: the water close by, small boats pulled up on the shore, and tired travellers climbing the steps to light incense in thanks for their safe arrival. Thian Hock Keng was built by people who had crossed the sea to start again. It has watched the sea retreat and a city rise around it, and it still opens its doors to anyone who wants to step inside.

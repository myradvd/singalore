Look up at the top of the building on this corner. Above the arches and the plain upper walls, at the front, sits an open pavilion with a curved roof in the traditional Chinese manner. It looks as if a small piece of a Chinese garden had been lifted onto a European church. That mix is the whole point of this place. This is Telok Ayer Chinese Methodist Church, the first church in Singapore set up only for Chinese Methodists.

The story starts with a doctor. Methodist missionaries first arrived in Singapore in 1885, led by William Oldham and James Thoburn. Two years later, an American medical doctor named Benjamin West came out with his wife. He taught for a short time at the Anglo-Chinese School on Amoy Street, then moved into Chinatown.

In 1889, West opened a clinic in a shophouse on Upper Nankin Street. That street no longer exists. Its site is now Hong Lim Park, not far from here. Soon he was using the same shophouse for Sunday worship. About thirty people came. Hokkien was the language they heard, and the church's records say some of them were West's own patients, men recovering from opium addiction. That small room is counted as the beginning of this congregation.

The early worshippers were mostly Hokkien and Foochow speakers. As the Foochow group grew, it left to start its own church, the Foochow Methodist Church. Those who stayed behind were mainly Hokkien, and the congregation came to be known as the Telok Ayer Church. In 1905, the shophouse was falling apart, and the church moved to Japan Street, which today is called Boon Tat Street.

Then, in 1913, the pastor, Reverend F. H. Sullivan, bought the plot you are looking at now, at the junction of Telok Ayer Street and Cecil Street. For a while there was no building at all. Services were held under a tent on the empty land, until the tent gave way. While a simple hut of wood and corrugated iron went up on the site, the congregation met each week at Fairfield Girls' School on Neil Road. The church's own history says the hut measured only about six metres by ten.

A permanent church was planned from 1921, after more land next door was bought. Bishop George Bickley laid the foundation stone on 19 January 1924. A year later, on 11 January 1925, his successor, Bishop Titus Lowe, dedicated the finished sanctuary. Some accounts give 1924 as the year of the building and others 1925, because the work spanned both.

The design came from the firm of Swan and Maclaren. The National Heritage Board names the architect as Denis Santry, the same man behind the Cenotaph, the Sultan Mosque and the old Tanjong Pagar railway station. The church's history names the builders as a French firm, Bross and Mogin.

Now take a closer look. The body of the building is Western. It is a rectangular block resting on rows of arches. Walk along the covered five foot way at ground level and look at the piers and columns. Their rounded tops carry small crosses, a detail borrowed from Byzantine churches. Many of the doors and windows are fitted with louvres.

Then there is the pavilion on the roof. The church's own history explains why it is there. A Chinese style roof on a Roman style building was meant to speak to Chinese migrants, so they could see the church as their own.

The church also sits upside down compared with many others. The ground floor holds a social hall named after Benjamin West. The sanctuary, where people worship, is on the second storey. That is unusual for a church here, though it was common in European city churches at the time. The church believes it may have been the first in Singapore to use this layout.

In its early years, the church did more than hold services. It helped members who could not read or write to send letters home to their families in China. In 1935, John Sung, a well known evangelist from China, held revival meetings here. The pulpit he preached from has been kept.

Then came the war. When Japanese forces attacked Singapore, the walls along the five foot way were made thicker, to stop stray bullets and shrapnel. Around three hundred people took shelter inside. One long serving member later recalled a small storeroom with a trapdoor, where women and girls hid from the Japanese. He also spoke of a telescope on the roof, used to watch for an attack that was expected from the sea. Through the Japanese Occupation, from 1942 to 1945, Sunday services carried on.

That sea used to be very close. In the early nineteenth century, Telok Ayer Street ran along the shore of Telok Ayer Bay, and boats full of immigrants landed here. From the late eighteen hundreds, the British reclaimed land from the bay. The street ended up four streets inland. Cecil Street, right beside the church, was built on that reclaimed land, along with Robinson Road and Shenton Way beyond it.

On 23 March 1989, the government named the church a national monument. That was the same year the congregation marked its hundredth anniversary. A careful restoration followed, from October 1993 to August 1995, at a cost of about three million dollars. A member who oversaw the work later said the original plans could not be found, so every detail had to be studied on site.

The restoration brought a surprise. Behind the altar, under wooden boards, workers found four old timber panels. They carry Chinese characters in gold leaf that read Shangdi shi ai, which means God is Love. Nobody is sure why they had been covered up. Around the walls of the sanctuary are six more characters. They stand for respect, piety, holiness, faith, benevolence and tolerance, values that belong both to the Christian faith and to Chinese tradition.

As the congregation grew, it built a second church on Wishart Road, off Telok Blangah Road, finished in 2004. But this corner building is where it put down roots. Telok Ayer Street holds six national monuments, and this church sits among temples, a mosque and a clan hall built by other migrants. Before you move on, look up once more at that pavilion roof, placed there a century ago so that newcomers from China would know this church was meant for them.

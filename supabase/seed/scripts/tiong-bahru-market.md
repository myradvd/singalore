Look at the building on the corner of Lim Liak Street and Seng Poh Road. It is made of concrete and has two storeys. Fresh produce is sold on the ground floor, and cooked food is served upstairs. The people who planned it gave parts of it an Art Deco look, to echo the flats around it. This is Tiong Bahru Market, and the building you see opened in 2006. The story of what stood here before is much older.

First, the names. Today it is Tiong Bahru Market. For a long time it was Seng Poh Road Market, and some sources call it Seng Poh Market. Seng Poh Road is said to be named after Tan Seng Poh, Singapore's first Chinese Municipal Commissioner.

The market exists because the neighbourhood grew. Tiong Bahru was a new housing estate built by the colonial Singapore Improvement Trust. Many of the families who moved in had come from crowded Chinatown, and they needed somewhere to buy food.

At first they had a temporary market. It was a row of sheds built in 1940. By the late 1940s it was an eyesore and a fire hazard, and it was far too small. One account also says that in 1945 two shophouses on Tiong Poh Road were turned into a wet market. Meanwhile the estate kept expanding. In 1948 the government began more than a dozen extra blocks, ready from the middle of 1949. In September 1949 came plans for eighteen more blocks across Tiong Bahru and the neighbouring Alexander estate. By December 1949 the municipality had decided that the estate needed a permanent, larger market along Seng Poh Road.

Building began in 1950. Most accounts say it was finished by the end of that year and open by 1951, though one account gives 1955, so dates differ. Either way, it was plain. It was a wooden structure with a zinc pitched roof. Meat was hung without refrigeration, and bargaining over prices was normal. It served not only Tiong Bahru but also Bukit Merah and Henderson, and its stalls sold everything from textiles to flowers. Crucially, it was built with space for cooked food stalls. It was meant to bring in the street hawkers who had been serving the growing community from the roadside.

To see why that mattered, you need the wider story of hawkers. Under colonial rule, officials saw street food sellers as a problem of crowding, dirt and disease. They began licensing hawkers. The rules covered wandering hawkers from 1915 and daytime stall hawkers from 1919. From 1928 the number of licences was capped at six thousand. It did not work. Shophouse owners, especially coffee shop owners, complained that unlicensed hawkers set up near their doors without paying rent or licence fees. By 1950, after the war and with high unemployment, there were around twenty thousand unlicensed hawkers. A Hawker Inquiry Commission was set up in April 1950, in the same year the building work started here. It proposed a licensing scheme with inspectors, but the scheme came three years later, and a proper Markets and Hawkers Department only arrived in 1957. The market was part of a larger search for an orderly home for the trade.

The market also did more than sell food. In its early days it served as a kind of town hall. In March 1951, residents gathered here to decide whether Tiong Bahru should have a community centre. It was a polling centre for the Legislative Council elections of 1951 and the City Council elections of 1953. In the 1960s, political rallies were held here too.

Singapore's policy on hawkers changed over the next two decades. In 1966 the Hawkers Code barred hawking on busy roads, in daytime car parks and near schools. A registration exercise finished in 1969 with about twenty four thousand registered hawkers. A committee to plan hawker centres was set up in 1971, and in 1972 the new Ministry of Environment took over the work. It announced ten new hawker centres by 1975. From 1971 to 1986, the government aimed to move up to eighteen thousand hawkers into markets and hawker centres with proper facilities. By the end of 1986 there were 113 hawker centres across the island. One heritage account lists Tiong Bahru Market among the early hawker centres built to serve housing estates.

By the 1970s the old market badly needed repair. It looked old and dirty, and street hawkers again lined its pavements. Yet its food was gaining a name. By then it was known for Chinese breakfast foods and local specialities, and there was only one Malay stall. In the 1980s it fed nurses from the general hospital in the morning, and office workers at lunch.

The old building was a single storey with a triangular plan. Its cooked food stalls ringed a courtyard, in a hawker centre behind the market that was officially called Lim Liak Street Hawker Centre, at Block 84A. When the whole place was revamped in 1993, it took the name Tiong Bahru Market. Around 2001 the National Environment Agency began a national programme to upgrade ageing hawker centres, and this market was among those upgraded.

The biggest change came between 2004 and 2006. The market and food centre closed, and the old building was replaced. Stallholders moved to a temporary site on Kim Pong Road during the work. The new building, completed in May 2006, has lifts, escalators and bigger stalls. That is the one you are looking at.

In December 2020, UNESCO added Hawker Culture in Singapore to its list of intangible cultural heritage. It was the country's first entry on the list. Hawker centres have been called the nation's community dining rooms. Long before that phrase existed, this corner was already a place where a neighbourhood shopped, ate, voted and argued about what it needed next.

Look up at the front of this building, on the corner of Telok Ayer Street and Boon Tat Street. Two square towers rise above a flat roof. Between them runs a strip of tiny arches and openings, like the front of a palace shrunk down to the size of a model. This is Nagore Durgha, also spelled Nagore Dargah. It began life as a shrine for a Muslim holy man, and it is now a national monument and the Nagore Dargah Indian Muslim Heritage Centre.

To understand why it is here, picture this street without the towers of the business district behind you. In the early years of British Singapore, Telok Ayer Street ran along the shore. Ships docked along this seafront, and many newcomers set foot on the island right here. The town plan of 1822 marked Telok Ayer out as a Chinese district, but the people who actually landed here came from many places. From the late 1800s, the British began reclaiming land from the sea. The street ended up four streets away from the water, and Cecil Street, Robinson Road and Shenton Way were laid out on the new ground.

Among those who came ashore were the Chulias. They were Tamil speakers from the Coromandel Coast of southern India, and in Singapore they worked mostly as traders and moneychangers. Many of them were Muslim. And many were devoted to a Sufi saint named Shahul Hamid, whose great shrine stands in Nagore, a town in Tamil Nadu. The saint was believed to have worked miracles, and he was especially loved by people who made their living on the water. Sailors and seafarers credited him with getting them home safely. Before leaving India for the long voyage to Singapore, Chulia travellers would visit his shrine in Nagore to ask for a safe passage.

Once settled here, they wanted a place to keep that devotion alive. So between 1828 and 1830, they built this shrine. At first it was called the Shahul Hamid Dargah. A dargah is a shrine to a holy figure, and only later did this one take the name of the saint's home town and become the Nagore Dargah. It is worth knowing that the saint is not buried here, and no relics of his body are kept inside. This building is a memorial, a way of honouring him far from Nagore. Inside there was a cenotaph shaped like a grave, and later generations came to treat it as a keramat, a sacred site.

The land itself came through a man named Kaderpillai. The records differ on the details. One account says he gave the land, while the heritage board says it was leased to him. The lease came with a condition from the British authorities. Nothing on the site could be built of attap, a palm thatch, or of wood, because of the risk of fire. According to one account, two brothers, Mohammed and Haja Mohiddeen, brought in building materials from overseas, including limestone, concrete and steel supports. The result was a solid masonry building, and today its walls are painted in peach and white.

Now take a closer look at the details. The style is a mix, drawing on several traditions at once. There are minarets and crescent and star motifs of the kind you see on mosques. But along the walls you will also find fluted pilasters with Corinthian and Doric tops, borrowed from classical European building. The overall look is often described as Indo Saracenic, a style that blends Indian, Islamic and European forms.

The main door faces Telok Ayer Street, under a Moorish arch with a scalloped edge made of many small curves. Mouldings of leaves and flowers decorate the wall around it. Look at the two towers again. They are cut with small niches. On some religious festivals in the past, oil lamps were set into those niches. Then walk a few steps along the Boon Tat Street side. There you will see a row of tall French style windows, each topped with a half round fanlight.

The front towers have been part of this street view for a long time. They can be clearly picked out in a painting of the area made in 1856 by the artist Percy Carpenter, looking down from a hill called Mount Wallich.

Running a shrine meant looking after money and property, and that brought the courts in. In 1893, a court appointed five trustees to manage the Nagore Dargah together with the Jamae Mosque. A new panel was chosen in 1910, and it took on Al-Abrar Mosque as well. That mosque, which began around 1827 as a thatched hut, stands a short walk along this same street. The three sites formed an endowment, known as a wakaf, and in 1917 their care passed to the Mahomedan and Hindu Endowment Board.

After 1968, ownership passed to the Islamic Religious Council of Singapore, known as Majlis Ugama Islam Singapura, or MUIS. On 19 November 1974, the shrine was gazetted as a national monument.

The building had an unexpected second life in the 1990s. In 1994, the nearby Moulana Mosque was pulled down to make room for the UOB Plaza tower, and its worshippers needed somewhere to pray. For a time, this shrine served as their mosque, and it was even registered under the name Masjid Moulana Mohamed Ali. When a new mosque for them was completed close by in 1995, daily prayers moved there. By 1996, the old shrine had fallen into poor condition and was locked up to keep the public safe.

Its future was settled in May 2005, when MUIS confirmed that the building would become a heritage centre. After major repairs, it reopened on 29 May 2011, opened by President S R Nathan. The two keramats and other religious fittings were taken out during the 2010s, and a further round of work ran from 2013 until early 2015. The old prayer hall has been kept, though it is no longer used for prayer. The centre now tells the story of Indian Muslims in Singapore and what they gave to the country.

At the opening in 2011, the minister in charge of Muslim affairs, Yaacob Ibrahim, put it simply. This, he said, was where the Indian Muslims had landed, and where they had made their place in Singapore. Stand at the corner for a moment and look along the street towards the old shoreline. The sea has gone, but the shrine that travellers built to thank a saint of safe voyages still stands on the street where they came ashore.

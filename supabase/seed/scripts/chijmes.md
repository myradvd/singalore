Look for the spire rising above the walls at the corner of Victoria Street and Bras Basah Road. It belongs to a Gothic chapel, and around it sits a whole city block of courtyards, arcades and old school buildings. For more than a century this was the Convent of the Holy Infant Jesus, a Catholic girls' school and home for nuns. Today the same grounds go by a new name, CHIJMES, pronounced like the word chimes. The name begins with CHIJ, the initials the convent went by.

Many people simply called it the Town Convent, because it sat right in the middle of the colonial town. Its story begins with a house that is still standing.

Find the building with a rounded bay that curves out towards Victoria Street. That is Caldwell House, the oldest building on the site. It went up around 1840 and 1841 as a home for H. C. Caldwell, a clerk in the colonial administration. Its architect was George Drumgoole Coleman, the first Superintendent of Public Works in Singapore, whose other works include the Armenian Church. Notice the tall French windows with louvres. They were meant to pull air through the rooms in the tropical heat.

The house became a convent because of a French priest, Father Jean-Marie Beurel of the Paris Foreign Missions Society. He wanted proper schools for the town's Catholic children. In 1851 he travelled to France to find teachers, and he won the support of the Christian Brothers and of a teaching order of nuns, the Sisters of the Holy Infant Jesus. In August 1852 he bought Caldwell House for the sisters. Accounts say he paid for it with his own money.

The first attempt went badly. Four sisters set out with Father Beurel, but their superior died after months at sea, another fell seriously ill, and a third gave up her work on reaching Singapore. So a second group came out, travelling overland across Egypt to reach Penang. From there, four sisters led by Mother Mathilde Raclot sailed south. They reached Singapore early in 1854 and moved into Caldwell House.

They wasted no time. Within about two weeks they had begun teaching. There were two classes, one for girls whose families paid fees, and one for orphans and the poor. Mother Mathilde ran the convent for around twenty years before she left for Japan, and in that time it grew into one of the largest institutions of its kind in the region.

Look again at that rounded bay. For decades its upper room served as the sisters' lounge, where they sewed, read, wrote and marked their pupils' work. A line in French can still be seen on the wall inside. It echoes the words spoken to Abraham in the Book of Genesis, walk before me, and be perfect.

The sisters are also remembered for a small door on the Victoria Street side of the convent. Babies and young children were left outside it, and the nuns took them in. Many of the babies were girls. Some were girls born in a year of the tiger, which some families believed would bring bad luck. The door came to be known as the Gate of Hope. In the mid 1850s the convent set up an orphanage, often called the Home for Abandoned Babies, where these children were raised. The gate you can see on the grounds today is a replica. The original, and the orphanage, were destroyed in the Battle of Singapore in February 1942, when Japanese bombs fell on the convent and blew out stained glass in the chapel.

Now turn to the chapel itself. The first chapel here, from the 1850s, had become so unsafe that the sisters moved their services into Caldwell House and began raising money for a new one. The design came from Father Charles Benedict Nain, a priest at the nearby Church of Saints Peter and Paul. Work started in 1901, the building was finished in 1903, and it was consecrated in June 1904. As you approach the entrance, look at the stone plaques set into the walls on either side. They carry the names of the donors who paid for it.

The chapel is built in a Gothic Revival style. A spire five storeys high marks the entrance, with flying buttresses on either side. Inside, the stained glass was made in Bruges, in Belgium, by the craftsman Jules Dobbelaere. The windows at the far end show scenes from the life of Jesus, from the Annunciation to his finding in the Temple, while panels along the sides depict the Twelve Apostles.

Before you go in, look closely at the tops of the columns, both in the chapel and along the covered walkways outside. Each capital is carved with its own mix of tropical plants and birds. By one count there are 648 of them, and no two are said to be the same.

The convent kept growing. In 1933 it took over four old bungalows that had once been the Hotel van Wijk and opened Saint Nicholas Girls' School in them. During the Japanese occupation, about forty sisters, orphans and teachers were sent to a camp at Bahau in Malaya, where many died. After the war, the schools carried on.

In the early 1980s the government acquired the land, and the schools moved to Toa Payoh. The last mass in the chapel was said on 3 November 1983, and the building was then deconsecrated.

On 26 October 1990, the chapel and Caldwell House were gazetted as national monuments. Restoration began in 1991 and took more than five years, at a cost reported as around a hundred million dollars. A specialist from France's historic monuments service helped oversee the repair of the stained glass. In 1996 the grounds reopened as CHIJMES, with the chapel renamed CHIJMES Hall. In 2002 the project received a merit award in the UNESCO Asia Pacific Heritage Awards for Cultural Heritage Conservation.

Today the courtyards are given over to dining, and the chapel serves as a hall rather than a place of worship. But the plan of the old convent is still here. Walk the arcades, find the plaques by the chapel door, look for the replica gate, and remember the nuns who crossed a desert to teach in this place.

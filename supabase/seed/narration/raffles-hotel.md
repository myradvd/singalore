You are standing on Beach Road, in front of one of the best known hotels in Asia. Its white walls, deep verandas and tall palms have filled postcards for well over a century, and today it is a national monument. But Raffles Hotel began as something much smaller. It started life as a rented seaside bungalow, turned into a hotel with only a handful of rooms. This is the story of how it grew.

The house that stood on this site was built in the early 1830s as a private home looking out to sea. It came to be owned by Syed Mohamed Alsagoff, a wealthy Arab merchant and landowner, who rented it out. For a few years it was run as a small hotel by a man named Charles Emerson, and after his death it was used as a boarding house for boys from Raffles Institution.

In 1887 the lease passed to four Armenian brothers, Martin, Tigran, Aviet and Arshak Sarkies. They were already in the hotel trade, with the Strand Hotel in Yangon and several hotels in Penang. They named their new venture after Stamford Raffles, the British colonial official remembered as the founder of modern Singapore. Raffles Hotel opened its doors at the start of December 1887. Accounts differ on its size, but the most common figure is just ten rooms.

The timing was good. Steamships and the opening of the Suez Canal had made the long voyage from Europe to Asia shorter and more comfortable, and a new kind of traveller was arriving: the wealthy visitor who came to see the East for pleasure. Demand soon outgrew the little hotel. New wings went up around 1889 and 1890, a billiard room followed, and in 1894 the Palm Court Wing opened in a neighbouring building. By then the hotel had around seventy five rooms.

The grand building you see in front of you came next. It was completed in 1899 on the site of the old beach house, which was pulled down to make way for it. The design was by Regent Alfred John Bidwell of the architectural firm Swan and Maclaren, and it is three storeys tall in a Neo-Renaissance style. It was modern in a way guests noticed straight away. The hotel had one of the first complete electrical systems in Singapore, running electric lights, ceiling fans and call bells from its own generators. In a hot and humid port, a fan turning overhead at the touch of a switch was a real luxury.

Through the early twentieth century, Raffles became a place to be seen. Over the decades its guest list grew to include writers, film stars and royalty. The novelist Somerset Maugham, the poet Rudyard Kipling and the comic actor Charlie Chaplin are among the names linked to the hotel. Much later came Michael Jackson, and the Duke and Duchess of Cambridge. The hotel has never been shy about telling these stories, and it keeps them alive in its corridors and bars.

One of its favourite tales dates from 1902. A tiger that had escaped from a circus nearby was found sheltering in the space beneath the billiard room, and it was shot there. It is the kind of story that grows in the telling, and the hotel still enjoys telling it.

Another story belongs to the Long Bar. It was there that a bartender named Ngiam Tong Boon created the Singapore Sling, a cocktail that carried the island's name around the world. Today the drink is as much a part of the hotel's identity as its verandas.

The good times did not last. When Arshak Sarkies died in 1931, it emerged that the brothers had run up heavy debts. The Great Depression made those debts impossible to manage, and the family business went bankrupt. In 1933 a new public company, Raffles Hotel Limited, was formed and took over the hotel and its land. The name stayed. The family that had built it was gone.

Then came the Second World War. In February 1942, Singapore fell to Japanese forces. The hotel tells a story that, as the city fell, guests were dancing one last waltz, and that staff buried the hotel silver in the grounds of the Palm Court to keep it safe. Under the occupation, the hotel was given a new name, Syonan Ryokan. Syonan, meaning light of the south, was the name the Japanese gave to Singapore, and a ryokan is a traditional Japanese inn. In 1945, when the British returned, the hotel was taken back.

In the decades after the war, the hotel carried on, but it changed piece by piece. Wings were added and rooms altered. Even its famous entrance had been reshaped. The original cast iron porch at the front, put up in 1913, had been taken down in 1920 to make way for a ballroom. In 1987, in recognition of its place in the history of Singapore, Raffles Hotel was gazetted as a national monument.

Two years later, in 1989, the hotel closed for a major restoration that lasted until 1991. The aim was to bring back the look of its heyday in the years around 1915. The cast iron porch you see at the entrance today is a reproduction made during that work. An ornate cast iron fountain made by the foundry of Walter MacFarlane and Company, which had been rediscovered in 1989, was brought into the gardens. When the hotel reopened, every room had become a suite.

The hotel has changed hands several times since then. Between 2017 and 2019 it closed again for another major renovation, reopening in August 2019. Today it is run by the hotel group Accor and has more than a hundred suites.

As you stand here, picture the whole sequence. A bungalow facing the sea. A tiny hotel run by four brothers from a distant community. A palace of electric fans and long verandas. A wartime inn under another name. And finally a monument, rescued and restored. Raffles Hotel has been reinvented many times, but each version has kept the same name, and each has drawn travellers back to this spot on Beach Road.

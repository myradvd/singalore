Find the building with the great curved Chinese roof, sitting above a broad body of reinforced concrete, with a garden spread out in front of it. That garden is Yunnan Garden, and the building is the former library and administration block of Nanyang University. Today it is home to the Chinese Heritage Centre, a research institute of Nanyang Technological University devoted to the study of Chinese communities living outside China. To understand why that institute sits here, in this particular building, you need the story of the university that came first.

Nanyang University was known to almost everyone by its short name, Nantah. It was the first Chinese language university in Southeast Asia, and only the second university of any kind in Singapore. In 1953 the rubber magnate Tan Lark Sye, a leader of the Hokkien community, proposed a university of their own. He gave five million dollars himself. The Hokkien Huay Kuan, the clan association he led, gave more than five hundred acres of land out here in Jurong, which was then mostly rural.

What made the appeal remarkable was who answered it. Money came from wealthy merchants, but also from trishaw riders, hawkers, cabaret girls and washerwomen. The university was meant to train teachers for Chinese schools across Malaya, and to keep Chinese culture and learning alive in the region. For many ordinary families, giving to Nantah was a way of opening a door for the next generation.

Building work on the campus began in the middle of the 1950s. While it went on, the first students took preparatory classes at two Chinese secondary schools. In March 1956, the university flag was raised in a simple ceremony on the new campus to mark the start of lessons. The official opening came two years later, on 30 March 1958, and around fifty thousand well wishers are said to have turned up for it.

Now look closely at the building itself. Its architect was Ng Keng Siang, a local architect who also designed the Lim Bo Seng Memorial on the Esplanade. The style is called the Chinese National style, and a BiblioAsia article places it in a wider movement known as Chinese Renaissance architecture. The idea was to join a modern frame of reinforced concrete with a roof that is unmistakably Chinese. In the years around the end of colonial rule, buildings like this were a way of saying that a modern institution could also look like it belonged to its own community.

The details reward a slow look. Along the edges of the roof are rows of round eaves tiles and triangular drip tiles. They carry two Chinese characters, nan and da, the short form of the university's name. On either side of the central block, the side wings show their brick faces openly. When it was new, this was the university library. In 1966 it took on a new role as the administration block, and that is the name it kept.

Out in the garden, look for two more pieces of the old university. One is the memorial, a tower with a double tier Chinese roof, standing on an eight sided base with steps on all four sides. It marks the completion of the first phase of building, and the colonial governor, Sir William Goode, unveiled it in 1958. The other is a gateway arch bearing the university's name. The one here is a copy. The original arch, the old entrance to the campus, still stands some distance away in the housing estates of Jurong West, cut off from the campus by the Pan Island Expressway.

Nantah's life was never easy. In 1959 an official commission criticised its staffing, facilities and curriculum, and declined to recommend that the government recognise its degrees. Further reviews followed. In 1965 a committee chaired by the historian Wang Gungwu looked at the curriculum, and its recommendations led to protests and class boycotts by students. The university also widened its reach. A Department of Malay Studies opened in 1968 and drew Malay students to the campus.

By the 1970s, many students were attending English language schools, and enrolment at Nantah suffered. In 1979 the government invited the British academic Sir Frederick Dainton to advise on university education. He proposed bringing Nanyang University and the University of Singapore together. The merger went ahead in 1980, creating the National University of Singapore. Nantah held its last graduation ceremony that August.

For a short time the campus stood empty. Then, in the early 1980s, a new body called Nanyang Technological Institute moved in. Around the start of the 1990s it became a full university and took the name it has today, Nanyang Technological University. So the campus you are standing on carries two names in its history: Nanyang University, the Chinese language university the community built, and Nanyang Technological University, the English language university that grew up in its place.

The building in front of you was given a new purpose in 1995. On 17 May that year, the Chinese Heritage Centre was set up as a non profit body with an international board of business leaders, scholars and public servants. On the very same day, the replica arch in the garden was unveiled by the minister George Yeo. At the end of the 1990s, the library and administration building, the memorial and the original arch were protected together as a national monument, the highest level of protection a site can have in Singapore. Records differ slightly on the exact year.

In 2011 the centre was restructured as an autonomous institute within the university. It is described as the first and only university institute anywhere dedicated to the study of the overseas Chinese. It works as a research centre, a library and a museum, and its library carries the name of Wang Gungwu, the same historian who once reviewed Nantah's teaching. Its work documents the lives and identity of Chinese people around the world, and the history of Nantah itself.

There is a neat fit between the building and that work. Nantah itself was an overseas Chinese project, paid for by a Chinese community that wanted its own place of higher learning, far from China. The centre now studies communities like the one that raised this roof. Before you move on, look up once more at the eaves, and find those two small characters, nan and da, repeated tile after tile along the edge.

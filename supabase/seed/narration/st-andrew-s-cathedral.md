Look up at the spire rising above the grounds between Coleman Street and North Bridge Road. This is St Andrew's Cathedral, the main Anglican church of Singapore. It is the second church to stand on this ground, and the first one had a short and unlucky life. Its story, and that of the men who built its replacement, is worth hearing first.

When Stamford Raffles drew up his plan for the town in 1822, he marked out a plot here for an Anglican church. Nothing was built for over a decade. The local community had to raise the money first, and it was only in the 1830s that work could begin. Until then, Anglicans in Singapore borrowed a simple wooden chapel with an attap roof, run by the London Missionary Society near the corner of Bras Basah Road. That spot is now part of Raffles Hotel, a short walk from here.

The first church was designed by George Drumgoole Coleman, the architect and surveyor who also gave Singapore the Armenian Church. Bishop Daniel Wilson of Calcutta laid its foundation stone on 9 November 1835, and the building was finished within a year or two. Scottish residents had given generously to the building fund, so the church was named after Andrew, the patron saint of Scotland.

Coleman worked in a calm, classical style, and not everyone liked it. Some people grumbled that it looked more like a public hall or a college than a place of worship. So in 1842 a spire was added, under John Turnbull Thomson. Nobody fitted a lightning conductor. Lightning struck the spire in 1845, and again in 1849. By 1852 the church was judged unsafe and closed, and in 1855 it was pulled down. While a new church was built, the congregation went back to the old missionary chapel.

The building you see now was started under the Governor of the Straits Settlements, William Butterworth. Its designer was Lieutenant Colonel Ronald MacPherson, who held two jobs at once. He was the government's executive engineer, and he was also in charge of the convicts. That second job shaped the whole project.

From 1825, the British sent convicts from India to Singapore. Thousands of them worked on roads, drains and public buildings. They were trained as brickmakers, bricklayers and carpenters, and many of the early colonial buildings in this district were raised by their hands. MacPherson chose the Gothic Revival style. Then he simplified his own plans so that Indian convict labourers could build them more easily. Using convicts was also a way to save money. His full estimate came to around a hundred and twenty thousand rupees, but he reckoned that convict labour, and materials made by convicts, would cut the cash outlay to under fifty thousand.

Bishop Wilson came back to lay the new foundation stone on 4 March 1856. MacPherson is said to have taken ideas from Netley Abbey, a ruined medieval monastery in Hampshire, England. Inside, the piers of the nave are said to closely resemble the ones that survive there. MacPherson did not see the work through. In 1857 he was posted to Malacca, and the job passed to others, chiefly John McNair and the engineer John Bennett, with W. D. Bayliss of the public works office.

Money stayed tight. In 1859 a newspaper complained that the few convicts working as plasterers inside had been taken off the job. Even so, the church held its opening service in October 1861, and it was consecrated in January 1862. Most accounts give 1861 as the year it was finished. But one careful study found that the outside and the spire were still incomplete at that point. MacPherson's spire design was dropped and redrawn by a public works engineer named Jasper Otway Mayne. The spire was done only in December 1864, and the last work ended in April 1865.

Behind the drawings stood a convict named Babajee Rajaram, from Bombay. He led the team preparing plans for St Andrew's and other public works, and he is described as the first native draughtsman in Singapore. Convict labour here carried on until 1873, when the last Indian convicts were shipped away.

Now look at the walls. For more than a century, nearly every description of this church has mentioned Madras chunam. This was a polished lime plaster named after Madras, the Indian city now called Chennai. McNair later wrote a book about the convict system, published in 1899, and in it he described the mix. It was shell lime without sand, beaten with egg whites and a coarse sugar called jaggery, then thinned with water in which coconut husks had soaked. Once dry, the walls were rubbed with crystal or smooth stones until they shone.

It is a good story, and it may not be true. An architectural historian, Yeo Kang Shua, spent years studying plaster samples from inside the cathedral during repairs. He found no trace of egg white or coconut fibre. A newspaper report from 1949 described the old interior walls as grey, while true Madras chunam was prized for being very white. With money so short, and with plasterers pulled off the job, he argues the famous plaster was most likely never used here. He also admits that we can never be completely sure.

Now turn to the tower. The belfry, with its pointed lancet windows, has been compared to the one at Salisbury Cathedral. Below it is a covered carriage porch, built so that people arriving could step down out of the sun. The first church had a bell given in 1843 by Maria Balestier, wife of the American consul and a daughter of the American patriot Paul Revere. It was replaced by a set of bells in 1889, and the old bell is now kept at the National Museum of Singapore.

The church became a cathedral in 1870. Inside, three tall stained glass windows behind the altar honour Raffles, John Crawfurd, an early Resident of Singapore, and Governor Butterworth. Each carries the man's coat of arms. A cross on the column that holds up the pulpit is made from nails taken from the ruins of Coventry Cathedral in England, destroyed by bombing in the Second World War. A stone from Canterbury Cathedral, sent in 1936, is set into a pillar near the lectern.

War came here too. In 1942, as the Japanese advanced on Singapore, the nave was turned into an emergency hospital for the wounded. On 28 June 1973, the cathedral was gazetted as a national monument.

Before you leave, look around the grounds for a granite memorial topped with a Maltese cross. It remembers Ronald MacPherson, who designed this church and never saw it finished. An old road called Jalan Klapa was renamed MacPherson Road in his honour. Most of the convicts who built this church left no names behind. The building is their record.

Look for the two slim towers rising above the row of shophouses. Each is eight sided, and each is topped with a crescent moon and a star. Between them is the entrance to Masjid Al-Abrar, one of the oldest mosques in Singapore. The building does not stand apart on its own plot. It sits shoulder to shoulder with its neighbours, as if it were simply one more shop on Telok Ayer Street.

The mosque has more than one name. Al-Abrar is an Arabic word for people who are righteous and devout. It is also called the Chulia Mosque, after the community that built it. And in Tamil it carries a humbler nickname, Kuchu Palli, which means the hut mosque. That nickname is a clue to how it began.

The mosque was founded in 1827. Its first building was a small hut with a thatched roof and walls of woven sticks plastered with mud, a method known as wattle and daub. The land beneath it was given as a waqf, a religious endowment, by an Indian Muslim merchant whose name is recorded as Hadjee Puckery Mohamed Khatib bin Shaik Mydin.

So who were the Chulias? They came from the Coromandel Coast, the south eastern shore of India. The name Coromandel is a European version of Cholamandalam, meaning the realm of the Cholas, an old dynasty of that region. For centuries the coast was a great trading region, with a chain of busy ports. Tamil Muslim trading groups from those ports, with names such as Lebbai, Rawther, Marakayyar and Kayalar, became known across Southeast Asia as the Chulia merchants.

In early colonial Singapore, the Chulias were mostly traders and moneychangers. Many of them settled and worked in what is now Chinatown, living alongside Chinese and other Indian residents. For them the mosque was more than a place of prayer. It was also where the community met, talked and kept in touch with one another.

To picture their world, you need to move the sea. Telok Ayer Street began as a coastal road running along Telok Ayer Bay, and it takes its name from that bay. In the first years of British Singapore, the street ran right along the water. Boats moored in the bay and waited for fresh water, which was brought down by bullock cart from a well at Ann Siang Hill. Temples and mosques sprang up along the shore, built by Chinese and Indian Muslim settlers who wanted to give thanks for a safe passage across the sea.

The hut did not last. In 1850, work started on a brick mosque to replace it, and the new building was finished in 1855. That is the core of the mosque you see today, although it has been enlarged since.

Within a year of its completion, the mosque appeared in a painting. In 1856 the artist Percy Carpenter painted Telok Ayer Street as seen from Mount Wallich, a hill that once stood close by. In that picture the two minarets rise clearly beside Thian Hock Keng, the Hokkien temple a little way along the street. So these towers have been a landmark here since the middle of the nineteenth century.

The Chulias built more than one place of worship in the area. The Jamae Mosque on South Bridge Road was put up in the 1830s, and it too is called the Chulia Mosque. A short walk along this street is the Nagore Dargah, a shrine the community raised to a Sufi saint whom many sailors credited with their safe voyages. Together these three sites formed the Chulia wakaf, a shared endowment. Heritage records differ on when Al-Abrar joined the other two under a single panel of court appointed trustees. One account links it to the completion of the brick building, while others place it in 1910. In 1917, all three were handed to the Mahomedan and Hindu Endowment Board.

Meanwhile, the shoreline was being pushed away. From the late 1870s, Telok Ayer Bay was filled in to make new land. Hills along the coast, Mount Wallich among them, were cut down, and their earth was used to fill in the water. Sources give different dates for when this first scheme was finished, but by its end, new streets and buildings stood between Telok Ayer Street and the sea. The waterfront mosque had become an inland one.

On 19 November 1974, Masjid Al-Abrar was gazetted as a national monument, the tenth in Singapore. After independence it had come under the Islamic Religious Council of Singapore, known by its Malay name Majlis Ugama Islam Singapura, or MUIS.

The building you are looking at owes much to a major renovation in the 1980s. Until then the mosque had only one storey. An upper floor was added, along with a large jack roof, to make the prayer hall bigger. The open courtyard between the gate and the prayer hall was covered over, creating a gallery above. Accounts of the new capacity vary, from around 800 to 900 worshippers. Later, in the 1990s, the mosque bought the shophouse next door, and that space became a prayer hall for women.

Now take a closer look at the front. The mosque is three shophouses wide. Like its neighbours, it gives up its ground floor frontage to the five foot way, the walkway that links the shophouses along this side of the street, so the path passes straight in front of the mosque without a break. Above, a parapet runs the full width of the front. It once covered only the central section. Its row of small decorative battlements, called merlon cresting, resembles the cresting on the Sultan Mosque.

There is a quiet piece of luck in the plan as well. Every mosque must face the qibla, the direction of Mecca. Here, the boundary lines of the plot happen to match that direction, so the prayer hall could follow the street grid and still face the right way.

The style draws on Indo-Islamic architecture, and that is what sets the mosque apart from the shophouses around it. But there are European touches too. Inside, the prayer hall is lined with Doric columns in the Neoclassical manner. Tall French louvred windows have coloured glass set into the fanlights above them. And over the mihrab, the niche that marks the direction of prayer, is a panel of blue glass bearing a verse of the Quran in Arabic, which opens in the name of God, the most gracious and most merciful.

One more name on the map tells this story. Not far from here, in the financial district, is Chulia Street. It was once called Kling Street, a term the Indian community found insulting, and in 1921 it was renamed after the Chulias. That street and this mosque both carry the memory of the same people, the merchants from the Coromandel Coast who once worked a few steps from the sea.

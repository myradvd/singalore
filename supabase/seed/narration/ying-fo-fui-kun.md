Look down the length of Telok Ayer Street and ask yourself where the sea went. Today the water is a long way off. But this street began as a coastal road along Telok Ayer Bay, and it took its name from that bay. In 1822, Stamford Raffles set this area aside mainly for the Chinese community, and it became the main landing site for Chinese immigrants.

Before the land was reclaimed, boats moored in the bay while they waited for fresh water, which was carried by bullock cart from a well at Ann Siang Hill. Between 1878 and 1885, the bay was filled in with earth taken from coastal hills, including Mount Wallich. One account says the street ended up four streets away from the seafront. Another says that the great temple down the road ended up five blocks from the sea. Even around the 1900s, the beach of the Telok Ayer Basin was busy with shipbuilding, as workers repaired vessels and put together light wooden boats called tongkangs.

The building in front of you, at number 98, is Ying Fo Fui Kun, a Hakka clan association and one of the oldest in Singapore. One account says its clan house was among the first buildings in Telok Ayer, where the island's earliest Chinese settlements were found, and that the association began life as a temple.

To understand why it exists, think about what a newcomer needed. On stepping ashore, finding somewhere to stay and someone to help you find work would have been a priority. Some also stopped at makeshift shrines along the waterfront to give thanks for a safe passage.

Studies of Singapore's clan associations make the same point. Most early Chinese migrants arrived virtually penniless, with no work, no lodging and no friends. Clan associations answered those needs. They helped people settle and find jobs. They looked after the sick, the destitute and widows. They organised shared social and religious activities, and they stepped in to settle disputes inside the community. One of their most important services was funerals. One study counted 200 Chinese clan associations in Singapore, and more than ninety percent of them were set up before 1960. It adds that the British administration left the different communities to run their own affairs, which made such associations necessary.

Ying Fo Fui Kun was one of the earliest. A group of Hakka clansmen founded it in 1822, though one account gives 1822 to 1823. That is about three years after Raffles landed. The man who led them is known as Liew Lok Teck, also written Liu Runde. One account says he hoped it would be more than a welfare body. He wanted it to be a kinship bridge between the Hakka in Singapore and China.

The word Hakka means guest families. The Hakka reached southern China later than other southern Han Chinese groups, and they often had to settle on poorer land. In early Singapore, the Hakkas were one of five main Chinese dialect groups, alongside the Hokkiens, Teochews, Cantonese and Hainanese. There were fewer of them than the other groups, yet theirs was one of the first to found a clan association. Most Hakkas worked as woodcutters and carpenters, or as blacksmiths and goldsmiths.

The first building was a single storey structure, and it served Hakka from five counties of Guangdong province. The association's name, Ying He, reflects its founders' wish for peaceful coexistence among the local Hakkas. By 1844, the committee judged that first building to be dilapidated. Funds were raised, and the present two storey house was built on the same site. Records differ on the date. Another account says the clan house was constructed in 1881 to 1882. All agree that it has always stayed at its original site.

Now look at the front. Two Doric columns frame the entrance, with an arch between them. The arch now carries the characters for Ying He Hui Guan. For decades it carried the name of the Ying Xin School, which the association ran inside the building from 1905 to 1970. Another account spells it Ying Sin and gives 1971.

Notice the windows on the front wall. They are set higher than a tall man's reach. One account says this was likely influenced by Hakka tu lou, the round earthen dwellings of China, where few windows sit high above the ground so intruders cannot climb in. Another says the windows were placed high to discourage intruders.

The plan follows the style of clan and temple buildings on the southeastern coast of Guangdong. A courtyard sits in the middle, surrounded by an entrance hall, a main hall and corridors. Light from the courtyard brightens the main hall by day. Granite columns hold up the second floor, where most traditional Chinese buildings would use timber. The roof structure, however, is timber. On the second level is a hall called Jia Yuan Ting, or the Hall of Fine Destiny, whose wooden roof members are painted with landscapes and scenes from Chinese mythology and folklore.

The building honours Guan Yu, the deified general of the Three Kingdoms period, also called Guan Ti. He stands for loyalty and courage. Accounts differ on whether his altar is in the main hall or upstairs.

The house also holds nineteenth century stone tablets and carved boards. The oldest surviving item is an inscribed board from 1846.

The association reached beyond this plot. In 1875 it built an extension called Wu Cheng Fu Di next door, to hold the ancestral tablets of the Hakka community. It was demolished in 1980 to widen the road. In 1887 the association bought land from the British government for burials, as its membership grew.

A renovation in 1997 restored the carved beams. On 18 December 1998, the clan house was gazetted as a national monument.

Before you walk on, find the Nagore Dargah a little way along the street. Then come back to those high windows, and the granite, and think of a community that arrived late, settled on poor land, and built this house to look after its own.

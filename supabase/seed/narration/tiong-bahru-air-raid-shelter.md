Look along Guan Chuan Street at the long, curved block of flats. This is Block 78, and it is shaped a little like a horse-shoe. It straddles two streets, Moh Guan Terrace and Guan Chuan Street. The part you are facing has something the other blocks in the estate lack. Underneath it, below the shops and the homes, is a shelter built to protect ordinary families from bombs.

Tiong Bahru was Singapore's first big public housing experiment, run by the Singapore Improvement Trust, a colonial body that was the forerunner of today's Housing and Development Board. Work on the estate began in March 1936. By 1941 it held hundreds of flats and shops, and more than six thousand people lived here. The shelter is part of the story of how that community met the war.

To see why it exists, go back to 1939. Singapore had a few air raid shelters already, built in the mid 1930s at Bukit Tunggal in Novena. Then war broke out in Europe, and people began pressing the government for more shelters. The government hesitated. Most of the city sat on low, flat ground close to the water table, so digging deep shelters would be difficult and expensive. Instead, it encouraged people to build their own and offered plans.

The Improvement Trust took a quieter route. On 28 June 1939, it announced that the new block at Guan Chuan Street would have a basement floor. In normal times it would be a covered play area for children. In an emergency, it could readily become an effective air raid shelter. Think about that for a moment. The same space was meant for children's games and for hiding from aircraft.

Records differ on the dates. One account puts the construction of the block between 1939 and 1940, another between 1939 and 1941. Records also differ on size. One account says the shelter could hold up to 1,600 people, while a newspaper report from 1941 spoke of room for two thousand. What the accounts agree on is that it was large, and that it was solid. One report from 1940 describes concrete floors and walls nineteen inches thick. That is almost half a metre of masonry.

Another account says the space was made from three play pavilions and four garages. Together they covered around fifteen hundred square metres. Heritage researchers also think it may have served as a depot for the air raid wardens, the volunteers who looked after civil defence in a neighbourhood.

Now look at the ground floor of the block, near numbers 41 and 43 Guan Chuan Street. A former resident, Nicholas Tang, who grew up in Tiong Bahru, recalled that the original entrances were here, in front of a coffee shop. When the siren sounded, two doors opened. A ladder at each doorway led down into the shelter.

The siren did sound. On 8 December 1941, Japanese aircraft attacked Singapore. Another former child resident, Linda Koh, remembered the shelter being used that day. She recalled that the inside was roomy but dark, with no ventilation. By her memory there were fewer than a hundred people inside, because the estate was not yet crowded. They waited about twenty minutes until the siren stopped.

The shelter also saw one of the stranger stories of the war. Mary Pereira says she was born in this shelter during an air raid in 1942. The shelter had medical facilities, and a doctor was on site to attend to her. Decades later, she shared that story at the launch of a heritage trail for the estate.

Even so, one account says the shelter was seldom used, because Tiong Bahru was not a major target for Japanese bombs. That did not mean the estate escaped. Roofs of several flats were damaged by the bombing, and some residents recalled at least two bombs falling on the estate. Flats were painted in camouflage colours as the war drew close. Playgrounds elsewhere in the estate were turned into makeshift shelters too.

During the Japanese Occupation, Nicholas Tang saw people use this shelter during Allied air raids in 1945. After the war, the story of the space gets less glamorous. Windows were added when it became a storage area, and for a time it was used to dump rubbish.

It was not the only one. A shelter on Eu Chin Street was later converted into a community centre, and another outhouse near Eng Watt Street was used by young boxers for training. Some homes had shelters of their own. One, in Block 74 on Tiong Poh Road, was a strong wooden structure built inside a hall, able to hold about thirty people.

Today this is described as the last remaining pre-war civilian air raid shelter in Singapore. It is also described as the first shelter built into a public housing estate, and the only Improvement Trust building designed with one. Those claims are worth treating with care, since the wording varies between accounts. But the point holds. Most of the shelters of that time are gone, and this one is still under a block of homes.

The National Heritage Board brought the shelter back into view in 2012, opening it for guided tours to mark seventy years since the Battle for Singapore. A year later, a heritage trail for Tiong Bahru launched with ten markers, and this place became one of its stops.

Before you leave, run your eye once more along the curve of the block. Ordinary families lived upstairs, and shops sat at street level. Under all of it was a space planned in 1939 for the day the sirens came. For most of the war it stayed quiet. That quiet is part of why it survives.

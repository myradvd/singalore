Find the tall clock tower. Below it, two long classical buildings stretch out to either side, joined so neatly that they look like one. They are not. They were built more than forty years apart, for different reasons, and under different names. One half is the Victoria Concert Hall, once the Victoria Memorial Hall, with a statue standing before it. The other half is the Victoria Theatre, which began life as the Town Hall. Together they make up one of the oldest performing arts venues in Singapore.

Start with the older half. In the 1850s, the colony needed a proper public building. Plays and gatherings had been held in the Assembly Rooms, a simple structure with an attap roof that had fallen into poor repair. The foundation stone for a new Town Hall was laid in 1855, and the building was finished in 1862. It was paid for partly by the British administration and partly by public donations. The engineer behind it was John Bennett, and he chose a Victorian revival look with Italianate windows, then fashionable in Britain.

The Town Hall did many jobs at once. It held a theatre, offices for the municipal government and space for social events. Accounts differ on which floor held what. For its first fourteen years it also housed the Singapore Library. As the town grew, so did the demand for entertainment, and the building could not serve as both an office block and a stage. By 1893 the offices had moved out.

Now turn to the other half. Queen Victoria died in January 1901. Across the empire, people looked for ways to remember her, and in Singapore the answer was a new public hall. The Governor called for contributions, and both the government and the public gave generously. The foundation stone was laid in August 1902, as part of the celebrations for the coronation of King Edward VII. Building work ran from 1903 to 1905.

A first plan came from Major Alexander Murray and the Public Works Department. The design was then reworked by Regent Alfred John Bidwell of the architects Swan and Maclaren, and some sources credit the hall to him alone. On 18 October 1905, Governor Sir John Anderson declared the Victoria Memorial Hall open. Inside, electric fans cooled the room, so a large crowd could gather in some comfort.

Look up at the tower again. It rises about fifty four metres and ties the two halls together with a shared corridor. The Straits Trading Company donated the clock and its bells, but the gift took time to arrive, and the timepiece was only fitted after the tower itself. The clock is around four metres across, weighs about a tonne, and cost six thousand dollars. On 1 February 1907, the Duchess of Connaught set it going. The tower was also placed in line with Anderson Bridge, which carries the road over the river close by.

The fundraising for the Memorial Hall had brought in more than it cost, and the surplus went to the old Town Hall. From 1906 it was remodelled into a proper theatre, and its front was rebuilt to mirror the new hall. That is why the two wings match today. The building reopened on 11 February 1909 as the Victoria Theatre, with The Pirates of Penzance as its first show.

The style is Neoclassical. Notice the tall Corinthian columns and the triangular pediments above them, which now carry the State Crest of Singapore. Look too for oval shields marked with the letters V, R and I. They stand for Victoria, Regina and Imperatrix, Latin for Victoria, Queen and Empress.

Now the statue in front of the concert hall. This is Stamford Raffles, the British official remembered as the founder of modern Singapore. The bronze was made by the sculptor Thomas Woolner and first unveiled in 1887 on the open field then called the Esplanade, now the Padang, to mark Queen Victoria's Jubilee, fifty years after her coronation. Stray footballs struck it, and spectators climbed on it to get a better view. So in 1919, for the centenary of the founding of modern Singapore, it was moved here to Empress Place, set off by a curved colonnade and a pool.

War changed all of this. During the Battle of Singapore, the Memorial Hall became a hospital for people hurt in Japanese bombing raids. During the occupation, the Victoria Theatre became a centre of cultural life for the Japanese community, while performances went on in the Memorial Hall. The Raffles statue was taken down and stored in the museum, amid fears it would be melted for its metal. The colonnade did not survive. In 1946 the statue came back to this spot, without it.

After the war, these halls saw some of the key steps toward self rule and nationhood. War crimes trials were held in the Memorial Hall from 1946 to 1947. From 1948, it was used to brief election officials and count ballot papers. From 1953, the Rendel Commission held public meetings here as it reviewed the colony's constitution. In November 1954, the People's Action Party was founded in the hall. And in 1958, after a long renovation of the theatre, a song by Zubir Said called Majulah Singapura was performed for the first time in the Victoria Theatre. It would become the national anthem. In February 1963, the island's first pilot television service, Television Singapura, was launched here as well.

Some changes were made to the buildings themselves. A crown had once topped the copper dome of the clock tower. It was taken down after Singapore gained full internal self government in 1959. Then, after renovation in the late 1970s, the Memorial Hall reopened around 1980 under the name it carries now, the Victoria Concert Hall, and became the home of the newly formed Singapore Symphony Orchestra. In 1992, the theatre, the concert hall and the tower were gazetted together as a national monument.

The most recent transformation ran from 2010 to 2014. The facades were kept, but most of the theatre behind its front wall was taken down and rebuilt. Its seating fell from about nine hundred to around six hundred. The old passageway and air well between the two halls, closed off for years, were opened up again as an atrium. And a new crown, based on Queen Victoria's, was placed back on top of the tower.

Before you move on, stand back far enough to take in the whole front. On one side is a town hall from the 1860s, rebuilt to match its younger neighbour. On the other is a memorial to a queen, renamed for the music played inside it. Between them, the clock still keeps time over Empress Place, as it has since 1907.

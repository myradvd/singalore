Look up at the front of the building. High on the facade, four pale figures look down over Keppel Road. They are part of what was once the Singapore railway station, also called Keppel Road railway station, and now known as the Former Tanjong Pagar Railway Station. For almost eighty years it was the place where Malayan trains ended and began.

To see why it is here, start with the harbour. A dock company was formed at Tanjong Pagar in 1864 and built wharves. The area was renamed Keppel Harbour in 1900. Plans for a railway through Singapore, mainly to serve that harbour, began in 1899. Trains first ran in Singapore around 1903, and the terminus then was at Tank Road, near the Former House of Tan Yeok Nee. The line was later pushed south towards Tanjong Pagar and the wharves, but it was abandoned after nearby residents complained about the noise.

Then came the Causeway across the Johor Strait. Goods trains began using it on 17 September 1923, and passenger trains followed on 1 October, though one account gives 1924 for the opening. Before the Causeway, passengers and goods had to change at Woodlands onto a ferry to Johor Bahru, and then take a connecting train. Once the Causeway opened, traffic between Singapore and Malaya grew sharply, and the old station at Tank Road struggled to cope.

Plans to rebuild Tank Road were dropped. Instead, the British administration chose a site near the docks. That meant goods from the Malayan interior could reach the harbour quickly. At the opening, John Strachan said the new terminus had architectural features more worthy of the city of Singapore than the shed at Tank Road.

A new route was needed to reach the site. A nine mile deviation was drawn up mainly to stop trains running along Bukit Timah Road, which was prone to flooding. Construction of the station began on 7 May 1929. By August 1931, over 630 concrete piles had been driven into the ground. The old line was later taken up, and some of that land became roads, including Clemenceau Avenue. The station work was supervised by John Strachan, who was general manager and chief engineer of the Federated Malay States Railways.

The station opened in 1932. Sir Cecil Clementi, the governor, officiated. Most accounts give the date as 2 May, though one says the station was completed on 3 May. Either way, the building became the southern terminus of the Federated Malay States Railways.

The design came from three architects at the firm Swan and Maclaren. Their names were H. C. Atkin Berry, Denis Santry and D. S. Petrovich. They drew ideas from railway stations in England. Santry also designed the Cenotaph, the Sultan Mosque and the Telok Ayer Chinese Methodist Church.

The style is mostly Art Deco. Look for geometric shapes and stylised flowers in the decoration. Both are used generously.

Now return to those four figures on the front. They are marble sculptures by Angiolo Vannetti, a sculptor from Florence in Italy. Each is an allegory of one of the main parts of the Malayan economy. The themes are agriculture, industry, commerce and transport. Above them, you can see the letters F, M, S and R. They stand for the Federated Malayan States Railways.

The roofs hold a surprise. They are covered in circular and triangular tiles, called wa dang and di shui in Chinese. Tiles like these are usually found on Chinese buildings and temples. Here they sit on a railway station designed by European architects, and they carry stylised Chinese characters for long life. Borrowing local building elements in this way was another habit of Art Deco.

Step inside, if you can, and look at the central hall. It is 22 metres high and has no supporting columns, so nothing blocks the view. Overhead is a reinforced concrete barrel vault, described as a major engineering feat of its day, and the hall was lit and ventilated naturally.

Tall murals line the walls. They are made of approximately 9,000 ceramic tiles and were crafted by William Rowe of Doulton and Company, which is now Royal Doulton. They show the work that made Malaya rich. You can see rubber tapping, tin mining, rice growing and different kinds of transport. At either end of the hall, look for the coats of arms of the Straits Settlements and of the Federated Malay States.

Even the floor was designed with passengers in mind. The wall panels and original floor slabs were made locally, using rubber to deaden noise. Outside, behind the main building, the platforms were long enough for the longest mail trains. They were covered by roofs of reinforced concrete, shaped like umbrellas.

This was never just a place to catch a train. The upper floors of the main hall held a station hotel. Over the years the building also held a hair salon, a restaurant, and a pub and lounge called the Prairie Express. In 1936, a bookshop called the Habib Railway Book Store opened inside.

The trains themselves ran a long way. In its last years, the line offered six daily intercity services to Kuala Lumpur, and other trains reached states such as Kelantan, in the northeast of Peninsular Malaysia.

The future of the station was argued over for years. In 1990, Malaysia and Singapore signed an agreement about it. Malaysia would move the station to Bukit Timah, and the railway land at Tanjong Pagar would go to a jointly owned company, with sixty percent held by Malaysia and forty percent by Singapore. But the two sides read the agreement differently. Singapore said it took effect at once. Malaysia said it applied only after Malaysia chose to move. The question was settled in a joint statement on 24 May 2010.

The final chapter came in 2011. The station stayed in operation until 30 June 2011, though one account gives 1 July as the day services ended. Sultan Ibrahim of Johor drove the last train out. His grandfather had opened the Causeway in 1923, and the Sultan learned to drive a train, with training from the railway, before he took the controls. On the same day, Malaysia returned the land to Singapore, and the terminus moved north to Woodlands.

Before that, on 9 April 2011, the main building was gazetted as a national monument.

Take a last look at the facade before you walk on. The trains no longer come here, but the four figures are still in place. Agriculture, industry, commerce and transport were what the railway carried, and they still face Keppel Road.

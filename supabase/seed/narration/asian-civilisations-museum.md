Face the river and then turn around. The long, pale building with the pitched tile roofs and rows of tall shuttered windows is the Empress Place Building. Today it houses the Asian Civilisations Museum, often shortened to ACM. Two names, one structure, and a story that runs from a colonial courthouse to a museum about the whole of Asia.

Start with where you are. This is the north bank of the Singapore River, right by its mouth. Its classical front caught the eye of immigrants and visitors sailing into the harbour.

The ground beneath your feet is older than any of that. Archaeologists from the National University of Singapore and Nanyang Technological University have dug the open land between the building and the river. They report that people were already living and working here in the early fourteenth century, and that the spot stayed in use during the time of the Melaka sultanate. A Portuguese map printed in 1604 even marks an official at Singapore whose title meant lord of the harbour, a sign that foreign merchants were still calling at this port long before the British came.

The British arrived in 1819. Close by on this same bank, a statue marks the place where tradition says Stamford Raffles stepped ashore on 28 January that year, although historians still argue about the exact spot. From then on, traffic on the river grew heavy as trade expanded, and this bank became the place where the new colony would govern itself.

The building in front of you was part of that plan. The project was approved in 1855, but work did not begin until June 1864, because the money of the Straits Settlements had been pulled away into military spending. The design came from Major John McNair, the Colonial Engineer, who was also the Superintendent of Convicts. That second job matters. The building was put up by convict labour, and the National Heritage Board says those convicts were Indian. It was finished in December 1867, and one report puts the final cost at fifty three thousand dollars, a little over the estimate.

It was first meant to be a courthouse, and it did serve the colonial court at the start. How long the court stayed is a question where records differ. One heritage account says the court soon moved out because the rooms carried sound badly. Others describe an older courthouse here that was extended toward the river and used for decades. What everyone agrees on is that the original building quickly filled up with something else: offices.

Almost the entire colonial bureaucracy ended up here. The Secretariat, the Treasury, Public Works, the Land Office and the Audit Office all had rooms in the building, along with the Colonial Engineer and the head of the police. The Legislative Council met in a large room upstairs. People simply called the place Government Offices, and for a time it was also known as the Council Chamber and Public Offices. Because the Surveyor General worked here too, the building long served as the reference point for the map coordinates of Singapore.

As the colony grew, so did the building. It was extended several times, and the last major addition came in 1920. Each new wing followed McNair's original style, which is why the whole thing still reads as one design. Then, in 1907, the open square beside it was named Empress Place, in memory of Queen Victoria, who had also held the title Empress of India. Over time the offices took the name of the square, and Government Offices became the Empress Place Building.

Now look closely at the walls. The style is Neo Palladian, a classical manner built on order and balance. The ground floor is rusticated, meaning the plaster is cut into heavy blocks to look solid. Above it run arcaded verandahs and a facade with Roman Doric columns. Notice the many French windows and timber louvres. Inside, the plan is laid out symmetrically along a central axis, and many of the original plaster mouldings and cornices survive.

In the twentieth century the building became political ground. After the Second World War it was seen as the control room of government, and it sat at the centre of the push to replace senior British officers in the civil service with qualified local people. In 1956 David Marshall, the first Chief Minister of Singapore, gave a stirring speech here calling for a Malayan Merdeka, meaning independence.

The building also touched ordinary lives. Crowds came here to register as voters for the 1959 elections, when Singapore moved to self rule, and again to register as citizens after full independence in 1965. Into the 1980s it held the Citizenship Registry, the Immigration Department and the Registry of Births and Deaths, so for many families this was simply where official papers were made.

When the government offices moved out, the building was restored and reopened on 7 April 1989 as the Empress Place Museum, opened by Ong Teng Cheong. It focused on Chinese history and art, and its first show brought royal objects from the Qing dynasty. But the museum ran into difficulties and closed in 1995. Meanwhile, in 1992, the building was gazetted as a national monument.

The Asian Civilisations Museum itself began elsewhere. It opened in 1997 in the Old Tao Nan School on Armenian Street, with a focus on Chinese civilisation, and it traces its roots further back to the Raffles Museum of the middle of the nineteenth century. In March 2003 it opened here, by the river, and widened its scope. The Armenian Street building later reopened as the Peranakan Museum.

The idea behind ACM is a pan Asian one. It deals with the material history of China, Southeast Asia, South Asia and West Asia, the regions from which the different communities of Singapore trace their ancestry, and it tells that story through the lens of Singapore as a port city. One part of its collection fits this riverbank especially well: the cargo of a ninth century trading ship found off Belitung Island in 1998, with more than sixty thousand Tang dynasty ceramics made in China.

Before you head inside or move on, look from the shuttered windows down to the water. People of the fourteenth century, colonial clerks, voters in queues, and now objects carried across Asian seas have all passed through this one stretch of riverbank.

Look up at the cluster of rounded towers in front of you. Each one is wider at the top than at the bottom, and each is wrapped in ribbed, earthy concrete that looks almost hand moulded. The building soon picked up a nickname: the dim sum basket building, after the round steamer baskets stacked on tables in Chinese restaurants. Once you have heard it, it is hard to see anything else.

The building has gone by several names. When it opened in 2015 it was called The Hive. The university also knew it as Learning Hub South. In March 2026 it was renamed the UOB Innovation Hub.

Before looking closer, it helps to know where you are. This is the main campus of Nanyang Technological University, usually called NTU, in the west of Singapore. In 1955 a Chinese language university called Nanyang University was founded just to the south of this spot, on land donated by the Hokkien Huay Kuan, a clan association. In 1980 it merged into what became the National University of Singapore, and in 1981 a new engineering school, the Nanyang Technological Institute, took over the grounds. Ten years later, on 1 July 1991, that institute became Nanyang Technological University. If you walk south through the campus, the old Nanyang University administration building still stands, now home to the Chinese Heritage Centre and a national monument.

The layout of the campus around you was planned in the early 1980s by the Japanese architect Kenzo Tange, who later won the Pritzker Prize. His plan set buildings among large green spaces.

The new building came out of a competition, won by the London studio of the British designer Thomas Heatherwick. He is perhaps best known for the cauldron that held the Olympic flame at the London Games in 2012. This was his studio's first major building in Asia. It worked with a local firm, CPG Consultants, which led the construction. The project was part of a larger campus scheme, and it was described at the time as the first redevelopment of the campus in twenty years.

The brief was a question as much as a design problem. If students can find lectures, books and experts on the internet from their own bedrooms, why should they come to a university building at all? Heatherwick's studio answered that the campus now mattered most as a place to meet people. Its target was the old model of box shaped classrooms lined up along long, dim corridors. The studio wanted a building with no corridors at all, one that would make students and teachers run into each other as often as possible.

So look again at how the towers are arranged. There are twelve of them, each eight storeys tall, standing in a ring around a shared central atrium. There is no single front door. At ground level the building is open on all sides, so you can walk in from almost any direction and arrive in the same shared space. The atrium is naturally ventilated, so air moves through it without being sealed in.

The classrooms themselves are unusual. There are 56 of them, and they are rounded, with no corners. That was deliberate. A square room has a front, where the teacher stands, and a back. The designers wanted rooms where nobody sits at the head of the class, and where students work together around shared tables while the tutor acts more like a guide. Every room faces the atrium.

Between the classrooms are balconies, small nooks and garden spaces, roughly as much space again as the classrooms themselves, meant for stopping and talking. The balconies grow larger towards the top. Plants and trees were placed on the roof and on some of the circulation levels, a nod to Tange's green campus.

Now step closer to the walls. The studio chose reinforced concrete and then set out to treat it like clay, giving it detail and warmth that people do not expect from concrete. The curved panels have irregular horizontal stripes, cast using ten adjustable silicone moulds. One architecture magazine thought the towers looked like giant parsnips. The designers preferred to compare the texture to wet clay. The towers are lifted off the ground on 61 angled concrete columns, and their surfaces ripple too.

Look at the concrete walls around the stairs and lifts, slotted in between the towers. They carry around seven hundred drawings by the British illustrator Sara Fanelli. They show images from science, art and literature. In 2013, before it was even finished, the project received Green Mark Platinum, the top rating in Singapore's green building scheme.

The building took 32 months to build and cost 45 million Singapore dollars. It was unveiled in March 2015, ahead of the new academic year that August. Its official opening came on 20 October 2015, when it was opened by Ong Ye Kung, then Acting Minister for Education. The building was tied to a change in teaching that NTU called the flipped classroom. Students watched lectures online before class, and time in the room was used for discussion, debate and team work. The university planned to move about 1,500 courses to this approach within five years. A second learning hub was already being built.

Not everyone was won over. Reviews were mixed. One British architecture magazine admired much of the building but found that parts of it felt like a lonely car park. Even so, The Hive went on to draw a steady stream of visitors with cameras, and it was shortlisted at the World Architecture Festival in 2015.

The latest chapter began on 25 March 2026. The building took the name of the bank UOB, and its lower floors were turned into subsidised offices for start ups linked to the university, backed by money from UOB and the Wee Foundation. The change was not quiet. Many students, especially from the humanities and social sciences, the building's main users, objected to losing rooms and to losing the old name.

Before you leave, stand in the middle of the atrium and turn slowly in a full circle. Twelve towers, no front, no back, no corridor. Whatever the sign outside says, the building was designed around one idea: that a university is most useful when people keep bumping into each other.

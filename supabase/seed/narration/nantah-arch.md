Find the gateway with the sweeping Chinese roof at the edge of Yunnan Garden. Start with the most useful fact. The arch in front of you is a copy. The original still stands, but not on this campus. It is on the other side of the Pan-Island Expressway, at Jurong West Street 93, among the housing blocks. This one was built because the original ended up cut off from the campus. It was unveiled on 17 May 1995 by George Yeo, who was then Minister for Information and the Arts.

So why build a second arch at all? To answer that, you need the story of the university it once welcomed people into. Its name was Nanyang University, and most people knew it by a shorter name, Nantah.

In 1953, a Hokkien rubber magnate and philanthropist named Tan Lark Sye proposed that the Chinese community should have a university of its own, one that taught in Chinese. He was then leading the Singapore Hokkien Huay Kuan, the Hokkien clan association, and he backed the idea with a personal gift of five million dollars. The clan association gave a large piece of land in Jurong, much of it a rubber plantation, as the site. Records differ on its exact size, but it was hundreds of acres in the west of the island, then mostly undeveloped.

Then came the fundraising. The money did not only come from the rich. Trishaw riders, cabaret girls and washerwomen gave what they could. Taxi drivers and trishaw pullers gave charity rides. Dance hostesses held charity dances, and hawkers sold charity meals. The campaign reached well beyond Singapore, to the Malay Peninsula, Sarawak, North Borneo and Indonesia. The university was, in a real sense, paid for by a whole community.

Building began in 1954. The local architect Ng Keng Siang was appointed for the first phase, and the arch was completed around 1955. It was designed in what is called the Chinese National style. That style mixes old and new. A distinctly Chinese roof sits on top of a modern frame of reinforced concrete. Look up at the roof in front of you and you can see the idea. The shape is old. The structure underneath it is not.

On the original arch, the name of the university is set boldly across the central beam, together with the year the university opened. One account says the four large characters of the name were written by the noted calligrapher Yu Youren. The arch was the front door of the campus. Cars and buses once drove through it from Old Upper Jurong Road on their way into the university.

Before the campus was ready, the first preparatory lessons were held at two Chinese schools, The Chinese High School and Chung Cheng High School. Then, on 15 March 1956, the university flag was raised at the new campus in a simple ceremony, and teaching began that month with a first group of 584 students. The grand official opening followed on 30 March 1958. On that day, cars and crowds are said to have filled the roads all the way from here in Jurong to Newton Circus in town.

Nantah's life was not easy. For years its degrees were not formally recognised by the government, which only agreed to recognise them in 1968. As more students attended English language schools, the university came under pressure to change. In time, apart from Chinese language and Chinese history, its classes were taught in English.

The end came in 1980. On 5 April that year, Nanyang University merged with the University of Singapore, and on 8 August the combined body was launched as the National University of Singapore. The teaching moved away, and the campus here stood empty for a time. Over its life, Nantah had educated around 12,000 students from Singapore, Malaysia and further afield.

In the early 1980s, a new body called Nanyang Technological Institute moved onto the old grounds. Records differ on whether it started in 1981 or 1982. In 1991 the institute became Nanyang Technological University, the NTU around you now. It took over the old grounds and carries a similar name.

Now turn and look behind the arch. The building with the large Chinese roof is the former Nanyang University Library and Administration Building. The replica was placed here, in front of it, on purpose. If you look closely at the ends of the roof tiles on that building, you may spot the round eaves tiles and the pointed drip tiles. They are stamped with two Chinese characters, nan and da, the short form of the university's name. The same marks were used on the tiles of the original arch.

In 1998, the original arch, the library building and a memorial in this garden were gazetted together as a single national monument. Notice the wording. The protected arch is the one out in Jurong West, not the copy you are looking at. This replica stands in for it, and keeps its outline in the place where the old campus once had its heart.

The original has had its own recent chapter. Its green glazed roof tiles had faded and been damaged by around seventy years of sun and rain. A major restoration began in 2024. After consulting the National Heritage Board, the restorers replaced the old tiles with identical new ones. Some of the original tiles went into the care of the NTU Museum, so a few pieces of the first arch have come back to campus after all.

If you have time later, the original is worth the short trip. It stands in the same spot it always has. What changed was everything around it. The old campus land around it is now a residential area, and an expressway now runs between the gateway and the garden it used to open onto.

Before you walk on, take one more look at the arch in front of you. It is not old, and it was never the campus entrance. But it was built for a reason. When the original was cut off from the campus, people wanted a gateway they could still stand under here, beside the library, the memorial and the garden. That is what this copy gives them: a way to keep Nantah's front door in view.

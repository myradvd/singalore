Stand at the corner where Tiong Bahru Road meets Tiong Poh Road and look up. The pale block in front of you, with its rounded corners and long horizontal bands, was the first block of flats built on this estate. It was finished at the end of 1936, and it marks the start of a story that most people in Singapore now live inside: the story of public housing. This is Tiong Bahru Estate.

The name is a small history lesson of its own. It joins two languages. Tiong is usually explained as the Hokkien word for a tomb or cemetery, and Bahru is the Malay word for new. Put together, Tiong Bahru means something like the new cemetery. Long before anyone thought of flats here, this was a landscape of low hills, swampy hollows and graves on the edge of the town. Squatters lived among the graves in crowded wooden huts. Some kept pigs, and many worked as labourers.

In the early twentieth century, the centre of Singapore was in trouble. Chinatown was badly overcrowded, roads and drains were poor, and disease spread easily. The colonial government's answer was a new body called the Singapore Improvement Trust, created by law in 1927. Its task was to improve the town, and over time that came to mean building homes.

Tiong Bahru became the Trust's test case. The land was acquired in the late 1920s and then prepared over several years. Around two thousand squatters were moved off the site and their huts pulled down. Graves were dug up and moved elsewhere, the hills were cut down, and the earth was used to fill the swampy ground. Roads, drains and culverts followed. It was slow and costly work, and when it was done the Trust tried to sell the land to private builders. Almost nobody wanted it. The economy was only just recovering from a slump, and so in the middle of the 1930s the Trust decided to build the housing itself.

The architect most often credited with the look of the pre war blocks is the Trust's Alfred G. Church. What he and his colleagues produced was unlike anything else on the island at the time. The style is usually called Streamline Moderne, a late branch of Art Deco that loved speed and machines. Look for the curved corners, the flat roofs, and the long horizontal lines that seem to pull the building sideways, as if it were moving. Residents found their own comparison. The rounded forms reminded them of the control tower at Kallang Airport, and the flats picked up a nickname that means aeroplane houses.

The first blocks also kept something familiar. Their plan was a reworked version of the shophouse, with shops on the ground floor and homes above, along with back lanes, air wells and small courtyards that let light and air into the flats. Staircases wind down the backs of the blocks. Some window and door frames were fitted with green tinted glass, meant to soften the hard tropical light.

The first families moved in as that first block was completed at the end of 1936. There were eleven households. The rent was around twenty dollars a month or a little more, depending on the floor. That was steep for the clerks and labourers the scheme was meant to help, so the early estate tended to attract people with steadier incomes. Even so, it grew quickly. By 1941 there were 784 flats, along with fifty four tenements and thirty three shops, and more than six thousand people called Tiong Bahru home.

Then came the war. Even before the fighting reached Singapore, the planners had built shelters into the estate. One of them, at Block 78 on Guan Chuan Street, is described as the first air raid shelter built inside a public housing estate in Singapore. When the Japanese attack came, some roofs here were damaged by bombing. During the occupation that followed, the estate filled up. People moved in to escape harder conditions elsewhere, tenants sublet their rooms, and the number of residents is thought to have roughly doubled.

After the war, the Trust went back to building, and the second phase of Tiong Bahru looks quite different. By 1954 it had added 1,258 more flats, mostly on the northern side of the estate. These blocks follow the International Style. They are longer and plainer slabs, set among lawns and footpaths, with their severe lines softened by rounded balconies, round porthole windows and spiral staircases on the outside. The planners drew on the new towns being built in Britain, places such as Stevenage, Harlow and Crawley, with their idea of homes set among green space and everyday services. By the late 1950s, around seventeen thousand people lived on the estate.

Tiong Bahru was always more than flats. Its planners gathered a market, coffee shops, hawkers and a Chinese temple around a central area, and the estate is linked to several early firsts, including one of the first community centres in Singapore and the first polyclinic. It also had a reputation. Some of the flats were home to cabaret workers and to the mistresses of wealthy men, and the estate picked up a teasing nickname that translates roughly as the den of beauties.

The street names are worth reading as you walk. They honour Chinese merchants and philanthropists of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Roads here carry the names of Khoo Tiong Poh, Koh Eng Hoon and Seah Eu Chin. Kim Pong Road is said to remember Low Kim Pong, whose firm was once the largest seller of medicines on the island. Together, the signs read like a list of the people who helped build the colonial town that this estate was meant to relieve.

The Singapore Improvement Trust was later replaced by the Housing and Development Board. Tiong Bahru passed into its care, and over time many of the pre war flats were sold to the families living in them. Beside the newer towns, with their taller blocks, the old estate began to look modest and old fashioned, and for years people debated what should happen to it.

The answer came in 2003, when the Urban Redevelopment Authority gave conservation status to twenty blocks of the pre war flats. The decision recognised more than the buildings. It recognised the way of living they were designed for: green open spaces, covered walkways along the shop fronts, and shops and services on the ground floor, right below people's homes.

In the years since, Tiong Bahru has changed again. Cafes, bookshops and small boutiques have moved into the ground floor units, and flats that were once hard to let have sold for around a million dollars. Some visitors come for the coffee and hardly notice the architecture. But the architecture is the reason this place exists at all.

As you walk on, look for the small details: the curve of a stairwell, a round window, the name on a street sign. Each one belongs to the moment when a colonial trust, an old burial ground and an overcrowded town came together to try something new. What began here as an experiment became the starting point for the public housing that now shapes daily life across Singapore.

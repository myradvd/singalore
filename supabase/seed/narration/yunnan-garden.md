Find the stairs on the western side of the garden, near the humanities building, and walk down. At the bottom you meet a small Chinese pavilion with a green tiled roof and red beams. Past it, the ground opens up into lawns, bamboo, little streams and winding paths. This is Yunnan Garden. It was once the heart of a university that no longer exists, and today it sits within the campus of Nanyang Technological University.

The numbers give you a sense of scale. The garden and its surrounds now form a heritage precinct of about nine hectares. That is bigger than twelve football fields. Keep that in mind as you explore, because the paths will carry you a long way from where you started.

The name has spread far beyond these lawns. In Chinese, people often call this whole campus Yunnan Garden, or simply Nantah, the short form of Nanyang University. Outside the campus gates, Yunnan is also the name of a subzone of Jurong West, and there is a Yunnan Park out on Jurong West Street 93.

To understand why a garden like this sits out here, picture Jurong before the university came. Like much of rural Singapore, it was covered in plantations, mainly gambier and rubber. Jurong Road was once the only road leading to the south west of the island, and it had just two lanes. Villagers used its milestones to describe where they lived.

In 1953, a Hokkien rubber magnate named Tan Lark Sye proposed something new for the Chinese community: a university of its own. He led the Singapore Hokkien Huay Kuan, the clan association of the Hokkien people, and also chaired the Chinese Chamber of Commerce. He and other community leaders wanted a place of higher learning for Chinese educated students, and a way to keep Chinese culture alive in Malaya. The university was also meant to train high school teachers.

Tan gave five million dollars of his own money. He is said to have been inspired by the giving of another businessman, Tan Kah Kee. The Hokkien Huay Kuan gave the land, a huge parcel in western Jurong of around 500 acres, then largely undeveloped countryside. And then ordinary people joined in. Heritage accounts of the fundraising name trishaw riders, hawkers, cabaret girls, washerwomen and dance hostesses, alongside millionaires, all giving what they could.

Building began in 1954. The architect for the first phase was a local man, Ng Keng Siang. He worked in what is called the Chinese National style, which puts a distinctly Chinese roof on top of a modern concrete frame. You can see that idea repeated around this garden, in the curved roofs and the red and green colouring of the pavilions.

Lessons began around 1956. On 15 March that year, the university's flag was raised in a simple ceremony on the new campus. One memory from those early days is that on the day the university opened, Jurong Road was so jammed that nothing could pass, and the traffic jam was said to be the biggest Singapore had seen. The official opening came on 30 March 1958, when about fifty thousand well wishers gathered here.

At the centre of this garden stands the Nanyang University Memorial, a tower with a double tier Chinese roof, unveiled that same day to mark the end of the first phase of building and to honour the donors.

For more than two decades, this was Nanyang University. It was the first Chinese language university in Southeast Asia. It added a Department of Malay Studies in 1968, which brought in Malay students, and it gave jobs to many people who lived in Jurong. And this garden sat at the centre of its grounds.

But in 1980 Nanyang University merged with the University of Singapore to form the National University of Singapore. The last convocation here took place on 16 August 1980. For a short time the grounds stood quiet. Then a new technical institute, the Nanyang Technological Institute, moved in. In the early 1990s it became a full university, Nanyang Technological University, which is the campus around you today. In 1998, three structures from the old university were gazetted together as a single national monument. And in 2019, the road in front of the old library was renamed Tan Lark Sye Walk, after the man who started it all.

Decades later, NTU set out to renew the garden. The rejuvenated Yunnan Garden opened on 13 February 2020, though some of the finishing work ran on into the next year. More than a thousand new trees from over eighty species were planted. A boardwalk, small themed gardens and a new system to manage stormwater were added. Nanyang Lake, to the north, was expanded and joined to the garden by a waterfall.

Now, walk east along the paths. Look down as you go. The tiles are laid in octagons, hexagons and triangles. Somewhere near the middle, you may find a small culinary garden, planted with edible plants such as thyme, rosemary and peppers. The pavilions along the way are good places to stop in the shade.

As you head north, you reach the waterfall, about five point six metres tall, at the northernmost point of the garden. Water from Nanyang Lake pours over it into a lower pool, and from there it feeds the little streams you passed on the way in.

Stairs on the northern side climb to the level of the top of the falls. From there, paths lead around Nanyang Lake, with views over the water.

The story of this place is still being written. In March 2026, a book edited by Associate Professor Hee Wai Siam was launched under the title Yunnan Garden, then and now. It gathers more than thirty essays in Chinese by teachers, students and alumni, set beside old black and white photographs and new colour ones. A great grandson of Tan Lark Sye came to the launch.

Before you leave, climb those northern stairs and turn around. From up there you can take in the whole sweep of the garden in one view: the pavilions, the lawns, the streams, and the land that a clan association and thousands of donors gave so that Chinese speaking students in Jurong could have a university of their own.

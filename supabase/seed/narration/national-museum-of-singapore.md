Find a spot on Stamford Road where you can take in the whole front of the building. It is wide, white and almost perfectly balanced, and right in the middle sits a large dome covered in small overlapping tiles. This is the National Museum of Singapore. When it opened in 1887, it had a different name: the Raffles Library and Museum. It is the oldest museum in the country.

The story starts well before this building. In 1823, Stamford Raffles held a meeting about a college he hoped to found, and the idea of a library and a museum for Singapore came up there. For years, little happened. A subscription library opened at the Singapore Institution, the school now called Raffles Institution. Then, in the 1840s, the Temenggong of Johor presented two ancient gold coins to the library. That gift prompted a small museum inside the library, and an appeal for more objects.

The collection did not have a settled home. It moved to the Town Hall, the building that is now the Victoria Theatre and Concert Hall, and later to a new wing of Raffles Institution. In the 1870s the colonial government took the library and museum on and named the joint institution after Raffles. Part of the push came from London. An exhibition of colonial products had been held at South Kensington, in the building now known as the Victoria and Albert Museum, and officials in Singapore wanted a permanent display of their own.

The collections soon outgrew borrowed rooms. Around 1882 the decision was made to build, and the Colonial Engineer, Henry McCallum, drew up the plans. Work began in 1884. On 12 October 1887, the Governor of the Straits Settlements, Sir Frederick Weld, unlocked the front door. It was the Golden Jubilee year of Queen Victoria's reign. When it opened, books filled the ground floor and the museum took the floor above.

Now look at the front again. The style is called Neo-Palladian. It is a European manner that prizes symmetry, so the left side mirrors the right. Look above the windows and you will see pediments, the small triangular caps that frame them. At each end of the front block there is a much larger triangular pediment, and these carry the coat of arms of Queen Victoria. That was most likely a nod to the jubilee.

The columns and flat pilasters follow an old classical order of rank. On the ground level they are Doric, the plainest kind. On the level above they are Ionic, with scrolled tops. Inside, eight richer Corinthian columns hold up the dome.

The dome is the part most people remember. Its fish scale tiles are made of zinc. Inside, it rises about 27 metres above a round hall called the rotunda. Light falls into that hall through Victorian stained glass panels patterned with flowers and grids. There are fifty of them, and during the last major redevelopment every one was taken down, cleaned and repaired.

The early years were not easy. Animal specimens grew mould in the damp air. Termites ate into the roof until part of it became unsafe. And because the windows had no glass, dust from the street drifted in and settled over the displays.

Still, the museum kept growing. Its first purpose was natural history, and it filled with zoological specimens from Singapore and the wider region. There was talk of moving to a larger site, but this central location was thought too good to give up. Instead, a second block was built parallel to the first, behind it. It opened in February 1907, on the first day of Chinese New Year. A library wing followed in 1916, designed to match. From 1907 until 1974, the skeleton of a blue whale hung from the upper ceiling.

Around the turn of the century, the German born entomologist Karl Hanitsch led the institution. He pushed it towards scientific research, and he began collecting portraits, plans and photographs of old Singapore. That was the start of a history collection, which would one day become the museum's main subject.

During the Japanese Occupation, from 1942 to 1945, the museum took a Japanese name, Syonan Hakubutsukan. The collections came through the war largely intact, thanks to Japanese museum directors and British staff who worked to protect them.

After the war, the library and the museum began to go their separate ways. The library moved into its own red brick building next door, and around 1960, as Singapore governed itself, the museum shed the Raffles name and became the National Museum. Records differ on exactly when that name was first used, and some accounts tie it to independence in 1965. In 1972 the animal specimens left for the University of Singapore and the new Singapore Science Centre. From then on, the museum focused on history, ethnography and art.

More changes followed. After two years of restoration, the museum reopened in November 1990. In 1992 the building was gazetted as a national monument. In 1993 it was renamed the Singapore History Museum, and for the next thirteen years it carried that name.

In 2003 the museum left Stamford Road altogether, after more than a century on this site, and moved into temporary space at Riverside Point while the building was redeveloped. It returned on 7 December 2006, with President S R Nathan at the opening, and with its old name back: the National Museum of Singapore.

That redevelopment added a modern wing of glass and metal, which more than doubled the floor space. Its centrepiece is the Glass Rotunda, a fresh take on the old one. It stands 16 metres high and 24 metres across, a steel mesh drum wrapped inside a larger drum of glass. A glass passage links the new wing to the original building. It rises almost 11 metres, and it is one of the largest self supporting outdoor glass structures in the world. This passage is the only modern intrusion the Urban Redevelopment Authority allowed. Some of its beams are made of optical glass, so that people inside can still see the whole dome.

One last detail is easy to miss. The red brick National Library next door was pulled down in 2004 to make way for the Fort Canning Tunnel. Where its car park used to be, the museum built a theatre, and lined its walls with 16,720 red bricks made to look like the library's own, laid in a herringbone pattern.

Before you move on, look up at the dome again. It has watched over a reading room, a whale skeleton, a wartime renaming and a glass wing at its side. Each age of this building left a layer, and the names on the door changed with each one.

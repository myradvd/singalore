Look down Telok Ayer Street and you will see offices, shophouses and new buildings, but no water. Yet the street takes its name from Telok Ayer Bay. It began as a coastal road beside that bay, and this small building in front of you once faced the sea. It is called Fuk Tak Chi, and it is also known as Fook Tet Soo Khek. Today it is the Fuk Tak Chi Museum, but for well over a century it was a working temple.

Start with the name. Its Chinese name is given as meaning something like Tua Pek Kong temple by the seaside. Tua Pek Kong is an earth deity, also known as Dai Bak Gong in Cantonese. He is said to be popular with Chinese of all dialect groups. Here, he was the heart of the temple.

Now, the date. Sources do not agree on every detail, so it helps to be careful. One account says Cantonese and Hakka immigrants set up a simple shrine here between 1820 and 1824. Roots gives 1824 as the year it began. What seems firm is an old plaque. It is dated to the fourth year of the Daoguang Emperor, which is 1824, and it carries the words Zepi haidao, which mean benefitting the island. It is described as the earliest known artefact of the Chinese community in Singapore. So by 1824, people were already worshipping on this shore.

There is an older story too. One account says a modest shrine to Tua Pek Kong stood by the sea even earlier. In that story, the body of an elder washed ashore on the original coastline here, and local people raised money to bury him on the spot. As the place gained a reputation, immigrant groups pooled funds to build a temple. Treat this as tradition rather than proven history.

Who built it is also told in slightly different ways. Most sources say the Cantonese and Hakka communities together. One says the Cantonese built it first and the Hakkas joined later. One heritage account credits the Hakka alone. Either way, this was a shared place. It has been described as an early example of the Hakka and Cantonese communities working together.

Imagine the setting. In those years the sea reached close to this street. Telok Ayer was the landing place for many Chinese immigrants, and Telok Ayer Street was set aside for the Chinese community in 1822. The shrine's purpose was simple. Newly arrived immigrants came to give thanks for a safe passage. Keep that in mind as you look at the front of the building.

The shrine did not stay simple. Donations paid for a brick temple, completed in 1825 according to some accounts. Surviving inscriptions record renovations or expansions in 1854, 1862 and 1869. The 1869 work was paid for by Cheang Hong Lim, a Hokkien businessman, so support came from beyond the two founding groups. An inscription from 1870 records a walled platform built in front of the temple, where performances were staged as offerings to the deities. That platform faced the sea.

Three associations stood behind the temple. Kwong Wai Siew represented people from Guangzhou, Huizhou and Zhaoqing. Fong Yun Thai represented people from Fengshun, Dabu and Yongding. And the Kar Yeng Five Districts brought together people from five districts including Meixian. The temple looked after two cemeteries, Cheng San Teng and Loke Yah Teng. It also worked as a community association, with a role in welfare and in settling disputes.

Running a shared temple was not always easy. In 1886 a quarrel broke out between the temple's two keepers over how money was divided. It grew serious enough to involve the colonial Inspector General and the Protector of the Chinese. On 2 January 1887 both sides signed a contract. From then on, the Cantonese and Hakka communities took turns appointing the temple keeper, and the keeper for the year carried responsibility for profits and losses. The same system of taking turns is still used by the association that looks after the temple's heritage today.

Meanwhile, the shoreline was disappearing. In the 1860s local businessmen asked the governor for permission to reclaim Telok Ayer Bay, and the request was refused. Reclamation went ahead between 1878 and 1885. Earth from nearby hills, including Mount Wallich, was used to fill the bay. Before that, boats had moored in the bay to collect fresh water, which was carried from a well at Ann Siang Hill by bullock cart. Afterwards, new land and new buildings stood between the temple and the water.

The twentieth century brought a different change. In 1985 the government requisitioned the temple. It officially closed in 1994. Taoist priests performed rituals to permanently remove the statues. The images of Tua Pek Kong and the City God, Cheng Huang Ye, were moved to an association in Geylang. In 1995 the Urban Redevelopment Authority awarded the temple, along with a free school and other shophouses, to the Far East Organization for conservation and reuse.

That led to the building you see now. It was restored in 1998 by craftsmen from China and Malaysia and reopened as the Fuk Tak Chi Museum. It has been described as Singapore's first street museum, with artefacts about the lives of early Chinese migrants. In 2000 it became part of the Far East Square development. Thian Hock Keng, another old temple from the days when this street faced the sea, is a short walk along Telok Ayer Street.

Take a last look at the temple front. It is a small building with larger ones around it. What remains of the temple today is its outer structure and the inscriptions on its inner walls. The statues have gone. The sea has gone. But the building still holds a record of how two communities built a shared place on the shoreline.

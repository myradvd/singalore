Stand at the edge of the Padang with your back to the field, and look up. Two grand stone buildings stand side by side along St Andrew's Road. The one with the green dome is the former Supreme Court. The one with the long row of giant columns is the former City Hall, which began life as the Municipal Building. Both are national monuments, and since 2015 they have worked together as one museum, National Gallery Singapore. While it was being planned, the project went by another name, the National Art Gallery.

Start with City Hall, because it came first. In the 1920s, Singapore's municipal government was growing fast. Its job was practical: roads and bridges, water, gas, electricity and street lighting. It had outgrown its old rooms in the Town Hall, and in 1920 it bought land on this side of the Padang. The new Municipal Building was designed chiefly by the municipal architect Alexander Gordon. Building work ran from 1926 to 1929, and it was declared open on 23 July 1929.

Look at the front. Eighteen Corinthian columns, each three storeys tall, march across the facade above a broad flight of steps. Before land was reclaimed along this coast, the buildings facing the Padang formed a stately seafront, a show of imperial power that greeted ships sailing past. Yet the look is partly a costume. Behind the stone face is a steel frame, and the windows and rooms inside are plain and modern rather than richly decorated.

Now turn to the building with the dome. Its site once held the Hotel de l'Europe, at one time the grandest hotel in town. The hotel ran into money trouble and closed in the early 1930s, and the government took the land for a new Supreme Court. The design came from Frank Dorrington Ward, chief architect of the Public Works Department. He matched its height to City Hall, so the two would read as one civic front.

On 1 April 1937, the Governor, Sir Shenton Thomas, laid the foundation stone, said at the time to be the largest in Malaya. Beneath it lies a sealed time capsule holding six Singapore newspapers from 31 March 1937 and a handful of Straits Settlements coins. It is not due to be opened until the year 3000. Work was slowed by late deliveries of steel, as shipping was congested and Britain was rearming for war. The court finally opened on 3 August 1939, at a cost of around one point seven five million Straits dollars. It is often described as the last Neoclassical building of colonial Singapore.

Look up at the triangular pediment above the columns. That carved group is an allegory of justice by the Italian sculptor Cavaliere Rudolfo Nolli, the same man who supplied the columns for City Hall next door. In the centre sits Justice on a throne, holding a sword and scales. Two kneeling figures flank her, one asking for protection and one giving thanks. To one side are figures for deceit and violence, and lawmakers holding books. To the other is a bull, for wealth, and two children hugging sheaves of wheat, for plenty. Below, a long frieze shows scenes of early Singapore, including building work, rubber tapping, fishing, trade and the signing of the 1819 treaty that set up the British settlement.

The big copper green dome has been compared to the dome of Saint Paul's Cathedral in London. Underneath it sits a round room that was built as the court's law library. Inside, there was no marble. Corridors were laid with rubber tiles made in Singapore to look like marble, and they helped muffle footsteps near the courtrooms. The fine plaster in the main hall was done by Chinese craftsmen who had fled the war in China and had learned European plastering in Shanghai.

Within three years of the court's opening, Singapore was at war. When Japanese bombs began falling in early 1942, the Municipal Building opened its doors as a shelter. After Singapore fell in February 1942, the Japanese ran the city's affairs from inside it. Allied prisoners of war were gathered in front of it before they were marched to Changi. The court next door became the Syonan Supreme Court. In 1943, Subhas Chandra Bose, leader of the Indian National Army, rallied here for Japanese support to free India from British rule.

Then the tide turned. On 12 September 1945, in this building, Admiral Lord Louis Mountbatten accepted the surrender of Japanese forces from General Seishiro Itagaki. A victory parade filled the Padang the same day. In the years after, the Supreme Court was one of the places where Japanese soldiers faced war crime trials.

In 1951, when King George the Sixth granted Singapore city status, the Municipal Building became City Hall. Eight years later, it hosted one of the country's founding scenes. On 5 June 1959, Lee Kuan Yew and his eight ministers took their oaths in the City Hall Chamber, and he became Singapore's first Prime Minister. That December, crowds gathered outside to see Yusof bin Ishak installed as head of state, and they saw the new flag and heard the new anthem for the first time. On 16 September 1963, Lee stood on these very steps and read the Proclamation of Malaysia.

Both buildings were gazetted as national monuments in 1992. The courts moved into a new building directly behind them in 2005, and the old pair fell quiet.

The idea of turning them into a national gallery was announced that same year. A design competition followed, drawing 111 entries from 29 countries. The winner was studioMilou, working with CPG Consultants. Because the buildings were protected, key parts could not be touched, such as the facades, the room where the surrender took place, and Lee Kuan Yew's old office. Before construction, archaeologists dug on the site and found objects from the fourteenth to the nineteenth centuries, traces of trade long before the colonial town.

Work began in 2011. The two buildings were linked underground by a new basement and overhead by glass and aluminium bridges, built so they could one day be removed without harming the old stone. Above it all floats a broad roof canopy on slender, tree like supports, inspired by tropical leaves and filtering light into the space between the buildings.

The Gallery opened in November 2015. With about 64,000 square metres of floor space, it is the largest museum in Singapore, devoted to the art of Singapore and Southeast Asia from the nineteenth century to today.

A colonial city hall, a court of law, a wartime headquarters, a stage for self rule, and now a home for art. Each of those lives left its mark on this one long front facing the Padang.
